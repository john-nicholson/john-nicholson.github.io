Living the truth in the simple things

24 October 2026·1086 words·6 mins

At our men’s group this morning we spent time looking at truth. My notes finished with a simple thought: live the truth in the simple things. Looking back over the passages we discussed, that feels like the thread that holds them together. There is plenty to explore about what truth means, but there is also a personal question running through it: how does what I believe shape the way I live?

One of the things that came out of the discussion was the difference between truth as something absolute and truth as something personal or subjective. We often hear the language of “my truth” and “your truth”. I can understand the desire to have our experiences heard and taken seriously. We each bring a perspective shaped by what we have lived through. But if our own perspective becomes the final measure of what is true, what do we turn to when it needs correcting? Where is the reference point beyond ourselves?

The Bible gives us that reference point in God himself. In John 14:6, Jesus describes himself as the way, the truth and the life. Truth has a foundation beyond what I feel, what I prefer or what happens to be accepted around me. That is reassuring, because it gives us something dependable to guide our lives by. It is also challenging, because I have to allow for the possibility that my understanding needs to change. Believing there is absolute truth does not mean that I understand it perfectly.

In John 8:31–32, Jesus connects knowing the truth with continuing in his teaching. We often remember the promise of freedom, but it sits within that call to remain in his word. Following Jesus involves allowing his teaching to shape us, including where it challenges a habit, unsettles an assumption or asks something of us that we would rather avoid.

This also brought us to structure and discipline. In Matthew 28:18–20, the Great Commission sends us to make disciples of all nations, baptising them and teaching them to obey everything Jesus commanded. Helping someone come to faith matters enormously, and the commission carries us into the life that follows. There is teaching, practice and a shared responsibility to help one another grow. Learning to follow Jesus takes time, and it needs a place in the ordinary rhythms of our lives.

We talked about the sense that many young people are crying out for structure, belonging and purpose. I find that quite searching when I think about what we have been entrusted with as the church. We are a people gathered around Christ, with a shared life to invite others into and a calling that gives that life direction. As members of his body, we have a part in the provision God has made for people who are looking for somewhere to belong and something to live for.

That asks something of us too. It means making room for people in our lives, reading Scripture together, praying together and being willing both to offer guidance and receive it. Structure becomes helpful when there are people who will walk with us, encourage us and notice when we are struggling. Discipline grows through returning to these things, even when we do not feel like it. These are ways we learn to live the truth together, with grace for the times we get it wrong.

That gives weight to Paul’s words about speaking the truth in love. In Ephesians 4, the setting is a church growing towards maturity in Christ. Humility, gentleness and patience are already part of the picture before Paul reaches that familiar phrase. We need truth to become firmly grounded in Christ, and we need one another as we grow. Love shapes both the manner of our speech and what we hope it will achieve. The truth we are speaking holds us accountable too. I need to be willing to let God’s word challenge my position, even when I have arrived in the conversation quite certain that I am right.

It is a useful question to bring into a difficult conversation: am I helping this person grow towards Christ? There is another question alongside it: am I willing to receive truth with the humility I expect from someone else? It is easy to recognise the value of honesty when we have something to say. It can be considerably harder when we are the ones who need to listen.

Romans 12:2 takes that further, describing transformation through the renewing of our minds. The chapter begins with God’s mercy. Our response grows from what he has already done for us, and his work reaches into our thinking, our priorities and our everyday decisions. This gives us grounds for hope as well as a challenge. God is at work in us; we can bring him the places where our lives and our professed beliefs do not yet agree.

The discussion also raised the uncomfortable gap that can exist between acknowledging Jesus and the way we live afterwards. That deserves some care. We will fail, and pretending otherwise would make honesty impossible. There is a difference, though, between recognising our need for grace and becoming comfortable with leaving parts of our lives untouched. Confession, asking forgiveness and putting something right can themselves be expressions of faith.

This is where the simple things become quite searching. Living the truth might mean keeping a promise that has become inconvenient, refusing to pass on a story we cannot substantiate, or giving someone the patience we would hope to receive ourselves. It might mean admitting we were unfair in a conversation. These are ordinary examples, but they give substance to what we say we believe. Our words matter, and the way we live gives people a context in which to hear them.

John 3:21 describes coming into the light so that it can be seen that our deeds have been done through God. That keeps the emphasis on his work, even as it invites an honest response from us. We can come into the light with our failures as well as our progress, trusting him to continue changing us.

I think that is the thought worth carrying out of the room after a study like this. There is more to understand, and we should keep learning. There is also truth we have already heard that needs room in our lives today. Perhaps the place to begin is with something quite ordinary: a conversation, a commitment, an apology. What would it look like to live the truth there?
