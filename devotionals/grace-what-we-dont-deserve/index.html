Grace: What We Don't Deserve

20 September 2026·1238 words·6 mins

When we talk about grace, it can become one of those Christian words that we understand without always feeling the full weight of it. We know the definition: unmerited favour. Something given to us that we have not earned and do not deserve.

Perhaps the easiest way to understand grace is to remember what it feels like to receive an unexpected gift, particularly when you were expecting something very different.

When I was younger, I spent quite a lot of energy trying to be like the people around me. I could drink as hard as they could, party as hard as they could and generally try to prove that I could keep up. Some of it was fun, and some of the memories still make me smile, but there is always a cost.

As I got older, there were things I could look back on and know were simply not God’s path for me. Choices I would not make again. Moments where, however much I might explain the circumstances or point out that everyone around me was doing much the same thing, I knew better.

That is one of the difficult things about guilt. It is remarkably resistant to explanation. You can tell yourself you were young, point to the people you were with, or say that it happened years ago and surely you should have moved on by now. Yet somewhere underneath all of that remains a simple truth: I knew this was not right.

Scripture does not try to talk us out of that feeling by pretending we have done nothing wrong. Quite the opposite.

“For all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God…”
— Romans 3:23, NIV

That is not particularly comforting if we stop there. We have all fallen short. Not some unusually bad group of people somewhere else. Not simply those whose failures are more visible than ours. All of us.

But Paul does not stop there either.

“…and all are justified freely by his grace through the redemption that came by Christ Jesus.”
— Romans 3:24, NIV

Those two verses belong together: we have fallen short, and we are justified freely.

That is grace.

The answer to guilt is not convincing myself that I did nothing wrong. It is discovering that Jesus already knew I had, and grace was offered anyway.

Most of us understand transactions. If I do something good, perhaps I deserve something good in return. If I work hard, I expect to be rewarded. If I cause damage, I expect to pay for it.

Grace breaks the transaction.

Instead:

  • we prepare ourselves for the bill and receive a gift;
  • we expect rejection and find ourselves welcomed;
  • we prepare our defence and discover that the Father is already coming towards us.

Jesus paints exactly that picture in the story of the prodigal son. The younger son has taken his inheritance, left home and wasted it. Eventually he comes to his senses and heads back to his father with a speech prepared.

“Father, I have sinned against heaven and against you. I am no longer worthy to be called your son.”
— Luke 15:21, NIV

He is right about one thing: he has done wrong. The father never tells him otherwise.

But the son has also worked out what he thinks should happen next. He knows he has forfeited his position and expects that perhaps he can negotiate his way back into the household as a servant.

The father refuses the calculation.

He calls for:

  • the best robe;
  • a ring on his finger;
  • sandals on his feet;
  • a feast.

The son returns expecting to negotiate for employment and discovers that his father is restoring him as a son.

That is the scandal of grace. It is not that our sin suddenly becomes insignificant. It is that God’s mercy is greater, and grace gives us a status we could never earn.

Paul later writes:

“Therefore, there is now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus.”
— Romans 8:1, NIV

No condemnation does not mean there is nothing in our lives to regret. There may be choices we would desperately like to make differently, and there may be consequences that cannot simply be undone. Grace does not require us to rewrite our history until everything we did becomes acceptable.

Instead, it allows us to look honestly at our history without being imprisoned by it.

I can say, I was wrong, without needing to add, and therefore I am forever condemned, because Christ has already answered the second part.

Perhaps that is why grace is such a powerful antidote to guilt. Guilt continually drags us back into the courtroom and asks us to argue the case again. Grace points us towards a judgement that has already been dealt with at the cross.

Grace does something else too. It does not simply forgive the person we were and leave us exactly as we are.

Paul writes:

“For the grace of God has appeared that offers salvation to all people. It teaches us to say ‘No’ to ungodliness and worldly passions…”
— Titus 2:11–12, NIV

The same grace that releases us from our past begins to shape our future. We do not change in order to earn grace; we change because we have received it.

That difference matters.

If my relationship with God depends upon continually proving that I deserve to be there, then every failure becomes a threat. I have to hide it, minimise it or somehow compensate for it. But if I understand grace, I can bring failure into the light. I can repent honestly, learn and change, not because the Father is standing at a distance deciding whether I have finally earned my way home, but because He has already run down the road to meet me.

Perhaps some of us are still carrying things from years ago: decisions we made, relationships we damaged, seasons where we wandered much further than we intended. We know the cost, and we know we fell short.

The extraordinary joy of the Gospel is that God knows too.

And then He gives us exactly what we do not deserve:

  • mercy;
  • forgiveness;
  • welcome;
  • a place at the table;
  • the status of a son or daughter.

That is grace.

Read together
#

Read Romans 3:21–26, then Luke 15:11–24, paying particular attention to what the son expects to receive and what the father actually gives him.

A question to sit with
#

Is there something from your past that you have confessed to God, but are still trying to punish yourself for?

Grace does not ask you to pretend it did not matter. It asks you to believe that what Jesus did matters more.

Prayer
#

Father, thank You for grace.

Thank You that You do not ask us to pretend we have never failed, and that You already know the parts of our story we would rather forget. Forgive us for the times we have walked away from Your path, and help us to repent honestly while also receiving the forgiveness You freely give.

When guilt tries to pull us back into something You have already forgiven, remind us that there is now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus. Teach us to live differently, not because we are trying to earn Your love, but because we have already received it.

Thank You for giving us what we could never deserve.

Amen.
