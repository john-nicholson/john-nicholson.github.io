Isaac’s Wells: Finding Room, Still Following God

10 October 2026·1270 words·6 mins

Read: Genesis 26:12–33

There is a phrase connected with my parents’ decision to move roughly 400 miles that has stayed with me: “The third well’s a gusher.” That move was a big thing for me as a child, and I remember the excitement. But I also remember the years before it, when my parents had been through illness, redundancy and times when we had very little. I remember seeing God’s provision throughout those years, and I remember how hard they were. There is no doubt about that.

Looking back now, I think more about what my parents carried through that time, and the faith involved in making such a significant move after everything they had been through. My excitement was real, but so was the hardship that had come before it. Both belong in our family’s story. When I talk about God’s provision, I am remembering what I saw while life was still difficult.

That phrase brings me back to Isaac’s wells in Genesis 26. We often read the account as a journey through places or seasons: contention, opposition, then room to flourish. There is something helpful in that, but I want to stay with the story long enough to notice what happens both before those three wells and after them. Finding room is a significant moment, but Isaac’s relationship with God carries on beyond it.

Returning to what sustained the previous generation
#

Before the account of Esek, Sitnah and Rehoboth, Isaac reopens wells dug in Abraham’s lifetime. They had been filled in after his father’s death, and Isaac restores their original names. These wells were part of the provision on which his father’s household had depended. Reopening them was practical work: people and animals needed water.

There is a reflection here for those of us who have inherited something of faith. We may have grown up hearing stories of prayer answered, provision arriving or people stepping out because they believed God had spoken. Those stories matter, but there comes a point when we must turn towards God ourselves. Knowing where a previous generation found water does not remove the need to dig.

For me, that inheritance includes remembering my parents’ faith through illness, redundancy and lack. I saw God provide, even though those years were hard. I understand more now about the responsibilities they carried, and those memories shape how I approach the decisions and pressures in my own life.

Water can be found in a contested place
#

Isaac’s servants find fresh water, but the herdsmen of Gerar dispute their claim to it. Isaac names the well Esek, meaning dispute. Another well brings another quarrel, and he names it Sitnah, meaning opposition. The difficulty in these episodes is not that the digging produces nothing. They find water, and conflict follows.

I think that challenges an assumption we can easily bring to faith. We take a step we believe is right, then encounter resistance and begin to question the whole decision. We expected the reassurance of things becoming easier. Instead, there are disagreements, practical pressures or another decision we would rather not have to make.

Isaac’s experience gives us reason to pause before treating difficulty as proof that God is absent. The chapter has already described God blessing him, yet he still faces envy, displacement and contention. Equally, this is not a reason to assume every contested decision must be right. We still need prayer, wisdom and the humility to listen. The story simply refuses to let us equate God’s blessing with a life free of opposition.

Isaac moves on and his servants dig again. There is a quiet persistence in that which I find compelling. Moving away from a particular quarrel does not mean abandoning the need for water or the hope of provision. He continues the work without making each dispute the centre of his future.

Room to flourish
#

At the next well there is no quarrel. Isaac calls it Rehoboth, recognising that the Lord has made room for them and that they can be fruitful in the land. After the earlier disputes, there is space to live and work.

I can understand why this is the part of the story people hold onto. When life has felt squeezed by uncertainty or repeated setbacks, the thought of God making room speaks deeply. There can be real gratitude in reaching a place where the pressure eases and something has the opportunity to grow.

That is where “the third well’s a gusher” has such power as a remembered phrase. It gives language to hope after difficulty. Genesis itself does not describe Rehoboth as a gusher, and it does not promise that everyone’s third attempt will succeed. We need to let the encouragement remain rooted in the God who provides, rather than turn the number of wells into a timetable for our own lives.

For some of us, making room may involve a move. For others, the circumstances may remain familiar while responsibilities, relationships or opportunities change. This passage gives us a way to reflect on those experiences; it does not supply a map that every life must follow.

The journey continues
#

What catches my attention is that Isaac then goes on to Beersheba. Rehoboth is not the final scene. At Beersheba, God appears to him and reassures him of his presence. Isaac builds an altar, calls on the Lord and pitches his tent. His servants dig another well, and later report that they have found water.

I read that as an invitation to keep looking beyond the relief of improved circumstances. There is more to Isaac’s story than reaching a place without a quarrel. There is worship, a renewed assurance of God’s presence and, later, peace with those who had sent him away.

The text does not tell us that God specifically commanded the move from Rehoboth to Beersheba, so we should be careful about making that claim. What it does show is that finding room did not bring Isaac’s journey to an end. God still had something to say to him there.

Looking back at our family’s story, it is tempting to organise everything around the move itself: before we went, when we arrived, what happened afterwards. Yet a move is only one part of a life of faith. The deeper question continues wherever we live: how are we learning to trust God, recognise his provision and respond to his presence?

What are we carrying forward?
#

I want to remember my parents’ story with gratitude while leaving room for my own journey to take a different shape. Their experience encourages me to trust God; it does not tell me exactly how he will lead me. Isaac’s story offers that same invitation to pay attention, keep working and recognise the places where God has made room.

We may be restoring something we have neglected, working through contention or beginning to see an opportunity to flourish. Wherever we recognise ourselves, there is value in asking what faithfulness looks like here. Sometimes it will involve moving on. Sometimes it will involve staying and doing the patient work before us.

And when room does come, I hope we will receive it gratefully and continue to seek the God who gave it. There is still a life to live, still worship to offer and still more to learn of his faithfulness.

Father, thank you for the faith handed down to us and for the ways you have provided. Give us wisdom when we face opposition, patience for the work before us and gratitude when you make room. Help us to keep seeking you through each change of circumstance. Amen.
