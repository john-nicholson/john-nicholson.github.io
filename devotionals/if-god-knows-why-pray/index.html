If God Knows, Why Pray?

14 September 2026·2934 words·14 mins
Late Night Questions
After hours · a continuing series

Late Night Questions

My wife is a midwife, and some of the more difficult theological and ethical questions in our house have a habit of arriving after a late shift, usually when we're both tired and neither of us is expecting a theological discussion.

This series is an attempt to keep track of those questions: not necessarily to find a neat answer, but to look at Scripture, draw on writers who have helped me think, and work out what I actually believe and why.

Tonight's questionIf God already knows, why pray?

Key passages: Scripture Daniel 9:1–3 NIV and Scripture Matthew 6:5–13 NIV

Tap or hover over either reference to read the full passage without leaving the page.

The particular version of the question was slightly more difficult than that. We were talking about praying for people to come to know Jesus. Scripture tells us that God is patient, “not wanting anyone to perish, but everyone to come to repentance” (2 Peter 3:9). If that’s God’s desire, why do I need to ask him for it? Am I trying to convince God to do something he already wants to do? If God knows the person, knows their heart and knows the outcome, what exactly is my prayer adding?

Daniel does something strange
#

Daniel 9 gives us an interesting place to start because Daniel seems to face a version of exactly this problem. At the beginning of the chapter, Daniel has been reading Jeremiah. He understands from Jeremiah’s prophecy that the desolation of Jerusalem is to last seventy years (Daniel 9:2; Jeremiah 25:11–12; 29:10). Daniel isn’t praying because he is trying to discover what God intends to do. God has already spoken about what he intends to do.

The obvious response might be to put the scroll down and wait. If God is sovereign, and God has said that after seventy years he will restore his people, what possible difference can Daniel make? Except Daniel does almost the exact opposite:

“So I turned to the Lord God and pleaded with him in prayer and petition…”

— Daniel 9:3

It’s the “so” that catches my attention. Daniel understands the promise, so he prays. Knowing what God intends to do doesn’t make prayer unnecessary; it becomes the reason for Daniel’s prayer. That begins to challenge one of my assumptions about what prayer is for. If prayer is primarily about informing God of a problem or persuading him towards a particular course of action, Daniel’s behaviour doesn’t make much sense. But perhaps that’s because I’ve started with the wrong model of prayer.

God knows. Ask anyway.
#

Jesus makes the apparent contradiction even more explicit in Matthew 6. Before teaching the disciples how to pray, he tells them:

“Your Father knows what you need before you ask him.”

— Matthew 6:8

Then, almost immediately, he teaches them to ask. Give us our daily bread. Forgive us. Lead us. Deliver us. And, perhaps most relevant to the question we’re asking:

“Your kingdom come, your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven.”

— Matthew 6:10

Jesus tells us in the same passage that God knows what we need before we ask and that we should ask him anyway. More than that, part of the model prayer Jesus gives us is explicitly asking for God’s will to be done. That suggests prayer can’t simply be the transfer of information from us to God. I’m not telling an omniscient God something he has somehow missed. Nor does prayer make much sense as an attempt to wear down a reluctant God until he eventually agrees with me. Jesus describes the person we’re addressing not as a distant decision-maker who needs lobbying, but as “your Father.”

C. S. Lewis wrestled with this problem too. Across his writing on prayer he returns to the strange relationship between petition and providence: if God knows and God acts, in what sense can our requests really matter? Lewis doesn’t reduce prayer to a technique where the right amount of faith, or the right combination of words, produces the desired result. He is willing to leave some of the tension intact. I find that helpful because Scripture seems quite comfortable doing the same.

Prayer as participation
#

I wonder whether Daniel gives us at least part of the answer. Prayer isn’t simply asking God to change his plans; it is one of the ways he allows us to participate in them. There is a pattern here that extends beyond prayer. God could proclaim the gospel without us, but sends people to proclaim it. He cares for the poor, but repeatedly tells his people to care for them. He provides for people, while inviting us to be generous. God’s ability to accomplish something without us doesn’t appear to be an argument, in Scripture, for us doing nothing.

Perhaps prayer belongs in that same pattern. When I pray for someone to come to know Jesus, I’m not trying to persuade God to start caring about them. I’m bringing someone I care about before a Father who already cares about them more perfectly than I do. I’m asking him to act, but I’m also aligning myself with something I already know to be close to his heart.

Paul seems quite comfortable holding those ideas together. In Romans 10:1 he writes:

“My heart’s desire and prayer to God for the Israelites is that they may be saved.”

There is no sense that God’s desire for salvation makes Paul’s intercession pointless. It appears to do the opposite. Daniel takes that participation even further. His prayer isn’t “they have sinned”; it is “we have sinned.” He identifies himself with his people, confesses with them and asks God for mercy. Prayer doesn’t leave Daniel as a spectator waiting for God to fulfil a prophecy. It draws him into what God is doing.

Prayer changes things
#

There is a temptation at this point to tidy the problem up by saying that prayer doesn’t change our circumstances; prayer changes us. There is clearly truth in the second half of that. Daniel’s prayer changes his posture towards his people, and spending time praying for someone will almost inevitably affect the way I think about and behave towards them. But I don’t think Scripture allows me to accept the first half.

Prayer changes things.

James tells us that “the prayer of a righteous person is powerful and effective” (James 5:16). Abraham intercedes. Moses intercedes. Paul repeatedly asks churches to pray for him. The early church prays for Peter when he is imprisoned and, rather dramatically, Peter turns up at the door while they are still praying (Acts 12). These people don’t behave as though prayer is simply a reflective exercise designed to alter their own state of mind. They expect God to act.

Healing makes this even harder to turn into something purely metaphorical or psychological. Jesus heals throughout the Gospels. He doesn’t merely speak about the possibility of healing; it is a substantial part of his ministry, and he commissions his disciples to heal too (Matthew 10:1, 7–8). The pattern continues through Acts, and James tells a sick person to call the elders of the church specifically so that they can pray for them (James 5:14–16).

For me, then, the question isn’t really whether God heals or whether prayer can change circumstances. God heals. God intervenes. Prayer matters. I have enough reason in Scripture to believe that, and enough stories from people I know personally that I am not willing to explain it away. I have a friend who was given around six months to live more than twenty years ago. He is still here. Personal testimony alone doesn’t form the foundation for Christian doctrine, and I don’t pretend to know the medical or spiritual mechanics behind every story, but testimony matters. In one sense the gospel itself is testimony — good news about what God has done — and the witness of those who saw the risen Jesus sits at the heart of the New Testament. Experiences like this don’t establish doctrine by themselves, but they sit comfortably alongside what I already find in Scripture: God still acts.

That doesn’t make the subject easier. In some ways it makes it considerably harder, because I also know people who haven’t been healed. Our own family has lived with chronic illness for years. We have prayed, other people have prayed, and we continue to believe that God heals while living with the reality that the healing we would dearly love to see has not happened.

But I don’t think the answer to that tension is to weaken the first statement until the tension disappears. Nor is it to blame someone who remains ill for somehow failing to generate enough faith. God heals. We should pray for healing. And sometimes, having prayed, we are still waiting. Why that is deserves its own question rather than an easy answer here.

Exactly how God’s action in response to prayer fits alongside his sovereignty and foreknowledge is also something Christians have debated for a very long time. I don’t expect to resolve that in a late-night conversation with my wife or in a blog post. But I don’t need to understand all of the mechanics before I pray. Daniel certainly doesn’t. He knows what God has said, he trusts God’s character, and because of those things he asks God to act.

There may be more happening than we can see
#

There is another picture of prayer that has stayed with me for years, although it comes from fiction rather than theology. I love Frank Peretti’s novels, particularly the way he depicts ordinary Christians praying while, unseen by them, a spiritual battle is taking place around the events they are praying about. Someone is simply praying in a room, apparently doing very little, while Peretti allows the reader to see a completely different perspective on what that prayer might mean.

I’d be careful about taking that picture too literally. This Present Darkness and Piercing the Darkness are novels, not field guides to the spiritual realm. Conversations about spiritual warfare can quite quickly move from what Scripture actually tells us into elaborate systems built on speculation. Perhaps partly because of that risk, spiritual warfare isn’t a particularly popular subject in many of the church conversations I’ve been part of. But avoiding speculation shouldn’t mean avoiding the subject altogether.

Scripture is quite clear that there is a spiritual reality beyond what we see. Paul tells the Ephesian church that:

“Our struggle is not against flesh and blood…”

— Ephesians 6:12

He goes on to describe rulers, authorities and spiritual forces of evil. Then, having described the armour of God, he tells them to “pray in the Spirit on all occasions” (Ephesians 6:18). And strangely enough, Daniel gives us perhaps one of the clearest glimpses of this too.

In Daniel 10, he has been mourning and praying for three weeks when a messenger finally arrives. Daniel is told that his words were heard from the first day, but that the messenger had been opposed by the “prince of the Persian kingdom” until Michael came to help (Daniel 10:12–13). There is a lot there that I don’t pretend to understand, and perhaps that is important: Scripture gives us a glimpse, not a diagram.

I’m wary of pretending that I know exactly what is happening in the spiritual realm when someone prays. Scripture doesn’t give us enough information to construct the mechanics, and Peretti’s imaginative picture shouldn’t become our theology. But Daniel 10 does make it difficult to describe prayer as merely a psychological exercise which helps me feel differently about my circumstances. Daniel is praying in the world he can see while something he cannot see is happening at the same time.

Perhaps Peretti’s fictional picture has stayed with me because it gives an imaginative form to that mystery. I don’t know what happens in the spiritual realm when we pray, but Scripture gives me reason to believe that prayer matters beyond simply changing me. As the popular worship song puts it: when I fight, I fight on my knees. Perhaps there is more truth in that picture than we sometimes allow ourselves to talk about.

Prayer as a way of living
#

There’s a line attributed to Smith Wigglesworth that I’ve always loved. In one form, it goes something like:

“I don’t often spend more than half an hour in prayer at one time, but I never go more than half an hour without praying.”

There are plenty of stories about Wigglesworth, and some aspects of his ministry and particularly his approach to healing deserve a much longer conversation. But this description of prayer has always resonated with me. It describes prayer less as an event and more as a posture.

Prayer doesn’t have to mean finding thirty uninterrupted minutes, adopting the right position and working through a list. It can be the continuing conversation that runs through the day: asking, thanking, questioning, listening, interceding, sometimes simply saying help. Paul’s instruction to “pray continually” (1 Thessalonians 5:17) begins to make much more sense viewed this way. It isn’t necessarily an instruction to spend every waking moment in formal prayer. It is an invitation to a life in which the conversation with God is never particularly far away.

And perhaps this changes the original question. If prayer is simply a mechanism for submitting requests, then God’s foreknowledge creates a genuine efficiency problem. Why submit information he already has? Why ask for something he already intends to do? But if prayer is relationship, participation and — somehow — part of a spiritual reality larger than the one I can see, then perhaps efficiency was never the point.

Perhaps I’ve been asking the wrong question
#

I think part of my problem is that I’ve approached prayer as though it were primarily a mechanism. If God already has the information and already knows the outcome, what function does my request perform? Jesus starts somewhere else: “Our Father…”

My children regularly tell me things I already know. They ask me for things I already know they need. Sometimes they ask for things I’ve already decided to give them. The conversation isn’t made redundant by my prior knowledge, because transferring information isn’t the only reason we’re talking. There is a relationship between us. That analogy only takes us so far, of course. I’m not omniscient, and my relationship with my children doesn’t solve the theological problem of divine sovereignty and human agency. But it does make me wonder whether I’ve been trying to answer a relational question in purely mechanical terms.

Daniel discovers what God has promised, and so he prays. Jesus tells us our Father already knows what we need, and so he teaches us to ask. Daniel prays in one realm while Scripture gives us a glimpse of a conflict happening in another. And throughout Scripture people pray expecting that God will actually act.

Maybe prayer isn’t about giving God information at all. Perhaps it is about relationship with our Father, participation in what he is doing and engagement with a reality of which we currently see only a very small part. We’re not trying to persuade God to become good. We’re not reminding him of something he’s forgotten. We’re asking him to act, aligning ourselves with what he is doing and accepting his invitation to participate in it.

That doesn’t answer every question about prayer. In fact, it creates several more, and some of those are considerably harder. If God heals, why doesn’t he always heal? What do we do when we’ve prayed for the same thing for years? If God can bring good out of suffering, does that mean the suffering itself was somehow good? What does it actually mean to pray “your will be done” when his will isn’t what we desperately want?

Those are probably questions for another late night.

References and further reading
#

Daniel 9–10 — particularly Daniel 9:2–3 and 10:12–13. Read together, these chapters give a fascinating picture of Daniel understanding God’s purposes, responding in prayer, and then being given a glimpse of a spiritual reality beyond what he could see.

Matthew 6:5–13 — Jesus’ teaching on prayer. Particularly the tension between “your Father knows what you need before you ask him” and Jesus immediately teaching his disciples to ask.

Matthew 10:1, 7–8; Acts 3; Acts 9; James 5:14–18 — examples of healing, intercession and the expectation that God acts in response to prayer in the ministry of Jesus, the apostles and the life of the church.

Ephesians 6:10–20 — Paul’s description of spiritual conflict, the armour of God and prayer.

C. S. Lewis, writing on prayer — Lewis wrestles in several places with petitionary prayer, providence and the difficult question of how our requests can matter to an omniscient and sovereign God. Letters to Malcolm: Chiefly on Prayer is particularly relevant.

Frank E. Peretti, This Present Darkness and Piercing the Darkness — fiction rather than theology, but an imaginative depiction of prayer occurring alongside an unseen spiritual conflict. Useful as a picture, rather than a description of how the spiritual realm necessarily operates.

Smith Wigglesworth — particularly the saying attributed to him about rarely praying for more than thirty minutes while rarely going thirty minutes without praying, which captures prayer as an ongoing way of life rather than simply a scheduled activity.
