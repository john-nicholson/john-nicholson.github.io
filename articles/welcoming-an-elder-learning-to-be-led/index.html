Welcoming an Elder, Learning to Be Led

15 November 2026·1075 words·6 mins
When Good Arrangements Become Traps - This article is part of a series.
Part : This Article
Part : When Church Leaders Disagree

We recently welcomed Ian as a new elder in our church. I am thankful for his willingness to take on that responsibility, and the occasion got me looking again at what the Bible says about church leadership. As I read, I found myself thinking as much about what it asks of the rest of us as about the person being appointed.

When Steff came to help pray Ian in, he encouraged the church to trust and submit. That stayed with me. Welcoming an elder is a moment of encouragement and prayer, but it also means recognising someone whose leadership we are willing to receive. There is something deeply countercultural in that, and something important for our discipleship.

The character behind the responsibility
#

Reading 1 Timothy 3 and Titus 1, I am struck by how much attention Paul gives to the way a person lives. Faithfulness, self-control, hospitality, gentleness and integrity feature prominently. There is a responsibility to teach and hold firmly to sound doctrine, alongside questions about home life, money, temper and reputation beyond the church.

These qualities take time to recognise. Ability is often easier to notice: someone speaks well, offers a clear direction or seems capable of getting things done. Knowing their character requires a relationship that reaches beyond those public moments.

I find the attention to home life particularly searching. The people closest to us encounter a version of us that an audience rarely sees. I can prepare for a meeting or a talk; living consistently with my family asks something more sustained of me. These passages give me plenty to examine in myself.

What also strikes me is how familiar these qualities become when we think about the people who have authority over us. At work or in public life, we hope for leaders who tell the truth, listen carefully and exercise judgement fairly. We value someone who can make a difficult decision without humiliating the people affected by it. Yet confidence and visible success can still attract our attention more quickly when leaders are being chosen.

The biblical emphasis on character challenges those priorities. It asks us to look carefully at the person to whom responsibility is being entrusted.

Learning to receive leadership
#

Jesus gives that responsibility its shape in Mark 10:42–45, calling his disciples to greatness expressed through service and pointing to his own example. Peter develops the picture in 1 Peter 5:1–4: elders are to shepherd willingly, lead by example and remember Christ as the Chief Shepherd.

That is a substantial responsibility. Service includes difficult conversations, correction and decisions that may disappoint people. Receiving that care can be demanding too.

This is where Steff’s encouragement brought the question home to me. If we acknowledge that God has placed elders among us to lead and care for the church, that ought to affect how we respond to them. Agreeing to be led must mean something beyond supporting the decisions I would have made myself.

Hebrews 13:17 holds responsibility on both sides together. It calls believers to have confidence in their leaders and submit to their authority, while reminding us that those leaders must give an account. Our willingness to follow sits alongside their accountability for the care they exercise.

Submission requires discernment grounded in Scripture. The Bereans in Acts 17:11 received Paul’s message eagerly and examined the Scriptures to see whether it was true. I find that combination helpful: an openness to receive, with a commitment to understand and examine.

We remain responsible for taking Scripture seriously, raising genuine concerns and addressing wrongdoing. Equally, accountability cannot become my reason for resisting every decision that differs from my preference. Sometimes the question I need to ask is whether I have identified a real concern or simply found it difficult to accept somebody else’s leadership.

Iron sharpening iron
#

This connects with the church leadership reflections I have been developing around how good arrangements can become traps. Trusted relationships can make honest conversations easier, but familiarity can also make us reluctant to ask a difficult question. The way we handle that tension matters.

Proverbs 27:17 gives us the picture of iron sharpening iron. Both people have to engage, and both need enough humility to discover something about themselves in the encounter. It is easier to welcome challenge in principle than to receive it when it exposes a weakness in my own judgement or behaviour.

An elder may need to hear that something important has been overlooked. I may need to hear that my objection owes more to pride, habit or wanting my own way than I had recognised. Both conversations can help us grow if we are willing to listen and respond.

The purpose is to help one another become more faithful to Christ. That asks for care in how we challenge, honesty in how we answer and a willingness to work through what has been exposed. There is a difference between sharpening one another and simply wearing one another down.

Built through relationship and trust
#

All of this depends on getting to know one another. I cannot fully know someone’s character from hearing them speak or watching them lead a meeting. Shared life gives us opportunities to see how words and actions fit together, how we respond under pressure and whether we are willing to acknowledge our mistakes.

I also find it difficult to be open without trust. If I am unsure how someone will respond, I may bring them a carefully managed version of myself: the easier question, the less embarrassing difficulty or the opinion I can defend most comfortably. The conversations through which we grow require enough trust to speak about what is actually going on.

That trust develops through ordinary encounters: listening, following through, handling confidences responsibly and responding with care. Each conversation helps shape whether the next honest conversation becomes more possible.

So, Ian, I am thankful that you have been willing to take on this responsibility. These reflections have left me thinking about my own part in supporting you and our elders: praying for you, getting to know you better, listening openly and being willing to receive both encouragement and correction.

My hope is that we keep growing as a church where we know one another well enough to speak honestly, trust one another enough to listen and remain willing to be shaped by Scripture. Welcoming an elder asks us to put that into practice together, as people learning to follow Christ.
