What the PHP security warning means for your business

26 October 2026·1039 words·5 mins

This is a little outside my usual posts, but I felt it was important enough to write about. The recent press coverage and discussion around the PHP security issue have prompted conversations here in Suffolk about what it means for local businesses.

Those conversations have brought up some very practical questions: does this affect our website? Is our hosting company dealing with it, or do we need to ask someone? And what could it mean for the business if something goes wrong?

You shouldn’t need to become a technical expert to get clear answers. So I wanted to explain what’s happened, why it matters, and what to ask whoever looks after your website.

What has happened?
#

PHP is software that works behind the scenes on many websites, including sites built with WordPress, Drupal and Joomla. You may never have heard of it, even if your business depends on it.

On 21 October 2026, the PHP team released updates for two critical vulnerabilities that could let an attacker run code on an affected server without logging in. The team urged users to update immediately: temporary measures were not a complete solution.

On 23 October, further guidance addressed an additional risk involving an unusual combination of settings and application behaviour. Your maintainer should check that guidance alongside the main update.

On 26 October, PHP also issued revised Windows packages to fix a separate vulnerability in a bundled component called Brotli. Providers running PHP on Windows should check whether those packages are needed too.

That does not mean every website has been hacked, or that every website uses PHP. It does mean whoever maintains a PHP-based site should promptly check the latest official guidance and confirm the relevant fixes are in place.

Why does that matter to a local business?
#

Think about what your website does for you.

For a tradesperson, it might bring in enquiries. For a holiday business, it might take bookings. For a shop, it could support sales. For a charity, it might collect donations or help people find support.

If the site stops working, sends visitors somewhere unexpected or exposes information, the consequences reach beyond the website. There may be lost income, interrupted work and difficult conversations with customers.

A website can also appear to work normally while a security problem remains underneath. Looking at the homepage is not enough to establish that everything is safe.

The useful question is: who can tell you whether your business is affected, and what has been done about it?

What should you do?
#

Start with whoever looks after your website: your hosting company, web developer or IT provider. If different people handle different parts, ask them to agree who is coordinating the response.

You do not need to send technical instructions. Ask for clear answers to these questions:

  • Does this affect us? Does our website, or another service we rely on, use an affected PHP installation?
  • Has it been addressed? Have the necessary security updates been applied to the systems actually running our site, and has the latest PHP guidance been checked?
  • Is there any sign of compromise? What checks are appropriate, and does anything need further investigation?
  • Can we recover? Do we have a recent, usable backup, and does someone know how to restore the service?
  • Has the service been checked afterwards? Do enquiries, logins, bookings, payments and other important functions still work?

Ask for a short written confirmation of the outcome and any outstanding actions.

Updating WordPress or its plugins should not be assumed to update PHP underneath it. Your provider should confirm that the relevant layer has been dealt with.

Similarly, fixing a vulnerability and investigating a possible break-in are separate tasks. An update addresses the weakness; it does not, by itself, establish whether someone has already used it.

If nobody can tell you who maintains the site, that is the first gap to close.

Security is part of keeping the business running
#

For a small business, another maintenance bill can be hard to welcome. When everything appears to work, it is easy to wonder what you are paying for.

I find it more useful to think of security capability as something like an insurance policy: an investment in protecting the business from disruption and helping it recover.

The analogy has limits. Maintenance can prevent trouble, while insurance often helps with the consequences. Businesses need both the ability to reduce risk and a practical way to respond when something goes wrong.

That does not necessarily require a large IT department or an expensive new platform. It starts with knowing what you depend on, agreeing who maintains it, and making sure support and recovery arrangements match how important it is to your business.

Where AI fits into this
#

The same questions matter as businesses begin using AI.

An AI tool that helps draft a public social media post has a different level of responsibility from one connected to customer records, email or a booking system. The more access and authority we give a tool, the more we need to understand how it is managed.

Who can it act for? What information can it reach? Who looks after it when something changes? How do we stop it or recover if it makes a mistake?

AI can make it easier to create a website, automate a task or connect systems. The ongoing responsibility for those things still needs an owner.

This PHP warning is a useful prompt to ask those questions across the technology a business relies on, including the website that happens to be in the headlines.

Start with a conversation
#

If you run a business here in Suffolk and are unsure whether this affects you, start by contacting whoever looks after your website.

Ask whether you are affected, what action has been taken and whether anything remains outstanding. You should be able to understand the answer without learning how to administer a server.

That conversation is also an opportunity to ask a longer-term question: do we have the support in place to look after the technology our business now depends on?

Technical reference: PHP’s official security announcements, including the announcements dated 21, 23 and 26 October 2026. Guidance checked on 27 October 2026.
