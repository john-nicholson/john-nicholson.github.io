Human in the loop isn’t the same as human judgement

10 September 2026·1731 words·9 mins

“Human in the loop” has become one of the standard answers to concerns about AI. We do not entirely trust the model, so we put a person somewhere in the process to validate what it has done. On the surface that sounds sensible, and sometimes it absolutely is.

But I think it exposes a much older problem in the way we design controls.

I’ve spent a lot of time working with security and compliance teams, and more recently with OT security teams where getting controls wrong can affect physical assets and operations as well as systems and data. I’ve seen similar patterns in higher education and charities, where safeguarding, accountability and practicality have to coexist across both physical and digital boundaries.

Across all of those environments, one question has become increasingly useful: what decision are we actually asking the human to make? The presence of a person does not, by itself, create judgement. Sometimes we are asking someone to weigh incomplete information and competing risks. Sometimes we are asking them to provide context that the system does not have. And sometimes we are simply asking a person to execute a set of deterministic rules that could have been encoded in the control itself. We tend to call all three “approval”. They are not the same thing.

Take IAM approvals. If someone is deciding whether access is permitted based on a known role, a defined system, segregation-of-duties rules, data classification and a clear policy, then there may be very little judgement involved. In that case the process is really applying policy. A person may still be sitting in the loop, but the decision itself is largely deterministic.

That is very different from a manager being asked whether someone genuinely needs the access to do their job. The policy may tell us that the access is allowable, but the manager is supplying business context. A security team may then be looking at a different question again: does granting the access introduce a technical or regulatory risk that is not obvious from the role alone? Those are three different decisions, yet we often hide them inside one workflow labelled “approval”.

That is where I think a lot of control design starts to go wrong. We focus on the presence of an approver rather than the anatomy of the decision.

A useful way to think about this is as a spectrum. At one end are deterministic decisions, where the answer follows from known rules and available facts. If a request complies with a defined policy, there may be no value in asking a person to reproduce the same calculation manually. There may still be exceptions, but the normal path should not require judgement.

Then there are contextual decisions. The rules still matter, but they are not enough on their own. Someone needs to understand the situation, interpret additional information, or decide whether something that is technically permissible actually makes sense. Good tooling can help gather the facts, identify anomalies and present the relevant context rather than simply presenting another yes/no button.

Beyond that are judgement-based decisions. These involve uncertainty, competing objectives or risks that cannot be reduced to a simple rule. The answer may not be yes or no. It may be “proceed with an additional control”, “accept the risk temporarily”, “choose a different route”, or “we need more information before deciding”. That is genuine judgement because someone is weighing consequences rather than checking compliance with a predefined rule.

And then there are decisions that are collective or contested. A decision may be acceptable from a delivery perspective but unacceptable from an operational, safety or regulatory perspective. In those cases the problem is not simply that we need more approvers. We need clarity about who owns which risk, who has the right to decide, and how conflicts are resolved when two legitimate perspectives point in different directions.

That “who” question is important and, I think, often overlooked. Why is this person the approver? Do they own the risk? Do they understand the context? Are they accountable for the outcome, or are they simply in the workflow because the process needed someone to click approve?

If the decision is deterministic, putting a senior person in the loop does not make it more valuable. If the decision requires business context, sending it to a central control team may be equally weak because they may not have the information needed to make a meaningful call. The right question is not just whether a human is present, but whether the right person is making the right kind of decision with the right information.

A simple example everyone loves to hate is expenses. Most organisations have some version of the same process: submit a receipt, select a category, confirm that the spend is within policy, then send it to someone else to approve. If the amount is within a known limit, the category is permitted, the receipt is present and there are no obvious anomalies, what is the manager actually being asked to judge? Often, very little. Yet we still create queues, reminders, dashboards and escalation processes around that approval step, and then measure how quickly managers clear them. We become very efficient at processing a queue that perhaps should not exist in the first place.

There is also a psychological risk in the way we design these controls. If someone is repeatedly presented with a long list of simple yes/no decisions, particularly where the reason for the control is unclear, the natural behaviour is to process the list rather than think deeply about each decision. Mental fatigue plays a part, but so does meaning. If people cannot see the value in the decision they are being asked to make, it quickly becomes an administrative task rather than an exercise in judgement.

That changes behaviour. Some people will rubber-stamp because approving is the quickest way to clear the queue. Others will become overly cautious because saying “no” feels safer than taking responsibility for a decision. In the worst cases, a control function can begin to behave like a no factory: its visible output becomes the number of things it has stopped rather than the risks it has intelligently managed.

Measurement can reinforce this. Activity is much easier to count than outcome. We can measure approvals completed, requests rejected, reviews performed and queue times. It is much harder to measure whether the organisation is now safer, whether the control prevented a meaningful risk, or whether the same outcome could have been achieved with less friction.

Once activity becomes the measure, activity has a habit of becoming the goal. Teams optimise for processing more approvals, identifying more exceptions, or demonstrating that controls are being exercised. Taken far enough, there is even a danger that the system implicitly expects a certain number of “no” decisions simply to demonstrate that the control is working. That is classic local optimisation: the control function may look highly effective while the overall system becomes slower, less engaged and not necessarily any safer.

The better question is not how much control activity we performed, but whether the control reduced risk while preserving the flow of value. That requires a more mature conversation about outcomes, decision rights and the nature of the risk being managed.

This is one of the reasons I think there is a useful lesson to carry forward from DevOps and modern security engineering. The goal was never to remove controls; it was to stop treating every control as a manual interruption. Where the rules were clear, we increasingly encoded them. Where the boundary was known, we built guardrails around it. Where something moved outside that boundary, we surfaced it to someone who had the context and authority to make a real decision.

That creates a much healthier relationship between safety and flow. Safety does not have to mean slowing everything down. Done well, the control becomes part of the flow rather than a queue sitting outside it.

This brings me back to AI. As we build AI-enabled and increasingly agentic systems, trust and validation are becoming central design questions. “Put a human in the loop” is an understandable response, but it is not enough to tell us whether the system is well controlled. Before adding another human checkpoint, we should model the decision itself: what evidence is required, which parts are deterministic, what context needs to be understood, whether there is a genuine trade-off to weigh, who owns the risk, who has the authority to accept it, and whether this is one person’s decision at all.

AI can help across several of those layers, but that does not mean it should replace every form of automation. Where a decision is deterministic, the best answer may still be a conventional rules engine, workflow, policy-as-code check or other established automation. If we know that the same inputs should always produce the same answer, there is little value in introducing a non-deterministic model into that part of the process.

AI becomes more useful where context needs to be gathered, interpreted or summarised; where anomalies need to be identified; where the relevant evidence is spread across multiple systems; or where a recommendation needs to be formed from information that is harder to express as a fixed rule. In many cases the strongest design will therefore be a combination: deterministic tools enforcing the boundaries we understand, AI helping to assemble and interpret context, and people making the decisions that genuinely require judgement.

The AI may orchestrate or call those existing tools rather than replace them. An agent might invoke a policy engine, query an identity platform, run a calculation or trigger an established workflow, then use the results to decide what should happen next or what needs to be surfaced to a person. That separation matters for trust as well as efficiency. We should not ask a probabilistic model to reproduce a deterministic control simply because AI happens to be the newest component in the architecture.

The objective should not be to maximise either automation or human involvement. It should be to put each in the part of the decision where it adds value. That is ultimately what good guardrails do: they automate the boundaries we understand so that we can spend human attention on the uncertainty we don’t.

Because every policy decision we ask a person to make steals attention from the judgement calls that only they can make.
