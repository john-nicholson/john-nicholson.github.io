What DevOps Taught Me About Managing ADHD, Attention and Flow

31 August 2026·1145 words·6 mins

I was chatting recently with someone else who has ADHD about how I manage notes, tasks and to-dos. What surprised me was that the useful bit wasn’t really the tools. It was the process I’d gone through to get there.

I was diagnosed with ADHD relatively recently, and that gave me a useful lens for understanding why some of my productivity systems had worked for a while and then gradually collapsed under their own weight. I don’t want to dwell on the diagnosis itself here. What mattered was the reframing.

I realised I didn’t really have a problem with capturing things. If anything, I could capture too much: notes, actions, ideas, follow-ups, things to research, things I ought to do and things I might want to do later.

All of it could be safely stored somewhere. The problem was that a complete system was not necessarily a useful system.

The productivity version of too much WIP
#

This is where I started to notice a connection with some of the organisational principles I use in technology delivery.

In DevOps, Lean and flow-based delivery, we spend a lot of time worrying about work in progress.

Too much WIP creates queues. Queues create delay. Delay hides problems.

And once there is too much work in the system, starting more work often feels productive while actually making flow worse.

My personal task system was doing exactly the same thing. I had built an increasingly accurate inventory of everything I had not done. Tasks could remain open indefinitely. Old priorities sat alongside new ones, and things that had once mattered stayed visible long after the context had changed.

Because nothing was really forced out of the system, the list just became noisier. From a traditional task-management perspective, this was technically quite good: nothing was being forgotten. From an attention-management perspective, it was terrible.

The first improvement was not better organisation
#

One of the earliest changes I made was simply reducing the friction of capture.

I started using ChatGPT as a live note-taking layer. That meant I could capture things quickly and structure them afterwards rather than trying to listen, think, interpret, organise and document everything at the same time. That was useful in meetings, sermons, workshops and research.

Capture first. Structure later.

That eventually evolved into Obsidian becoming the durable knowledge layer, with Git underneath it and ChatGPT increasingly acting as the authoring and processing interface.

That part worked well. But it solved knowledge better than it solved attention.

Remembering everything turned out to be part of the problem
#

This was probably the more important realisation.

Knowledge benefits from persistence. Attention often does not.

I want decisions, context, notes and project history to survive. I do not necessarily want every task I have ever thought might be worth doing sitting in front of me every morning.

That distinction became increasingly important, and it led me back to one of the ideas from the Bullet Journal approach that I had probably underestimated before: migration.

Not migration as in copying yesterday’s unfinished tasks onto today’s page. Migration as a deliberate decision.

  • Do I still intend to do this?
  • Is it still relevant?
  • Does it belong to a project rather than today?
  • Am I waiting on somebody else?
  • Has the context changed?
  • Can I just drop it?

That is much closer to backlog refinement than task rollover.

Migration is really about re-establishing intent
#

This is the bit I now find most useful.

A task should not survive simply because I once wrote it down. It should survive because I still intend to do something about it.

That sounds obvious, but many digital task systems make persistence almost frictionless. Once something enters the backlog, it can live there forever. The result is the personal equivalent of an overloaded delivery pipeline: everything is in progress and nothing is really flowing.

So I now try to treat migration as a feedback loop: review the work, reassess the context, limit what is active, remove what no longer matters, then start again from a cleaner state.

That is very close to how I think about healthy engineering systems. Good flow is not created by pushing more work into the system. It comes from reducing queues, limiting WIP, making constraints visible and shortening the feedback loop.

The system I use now
#

What I’ve ended up with is less a productivity app and more a small personal operating model.

Obsidian holds durable knowledge, project context and history. Git gives me versioning and a reliable underlying store. ChatGPT acts as an interaction layer: capturing, structuring, reviewing and helping me reason about what is actually active.

Then there is a much lighter daily layer. That daily layer is intentionally disposable. Its job is not to represent everything; its job is to represent what deserves my attention now.

And periodically I migrate.

Not everything survives. That is a feature.

The principle underneath it
#

I think the most useful distinction I’ve arrived at is this:

Knowledge should persist. Attention should be disposable.

The knowledge system can be deep. The active work system should be shallow.

That separation has helped me far more than trying to build one perfect tool that remembers everything, prioritises everything and presents everything at once.

It also reflects something I’ve believed for a long time in organisational design. If work is not flowing, telling people to work harder is rarely the answer. You look at the system:

  • Where are the queues?
  • Where is the cognitive load?
  • Where are hand-offs creating friction?
  • What work has entered the system without a clear exit?
  • Where has “important” become indistinguishable from “still open”?

That turns out to be a useful way to think about personal productivity too.

Design for recovery, not perfection
#

The other lesson is that the system needs to work when I am not operating at my best.

If a productivity system only works when I am highly organised, fully focused and consistently disciplined, it is not much of a system.

For me, the more useful design goal has become:

How quickly can I restart?

  • After a busy week.
  • After travel.
  • After getting distracted by something more interesting.
  • After letting the task list drift.

A good system should make recovery cheap.

That is probably the most DevOps-like principle of all. Don’t design for the assumption that nothing will go wrong. Design so that recovery is normal, fast and low-friction.

That is roughly where I have landed.

Not a perfect productivity system.

Just a set of feedback loops, constraints and external memory that make it easier for me to get back into flow.

A note on AI: I’ve used ChatGPT as part of the process of writing this article — mainly to help me reconstruct the journey, organise the notes and turn the thinking into a coherent draft. The underlying experiences, views and conclusions are mine.
