Building a Publishing System That Helps Me Think

Over the last few weeks I’ve been writing more.

That has created a slightly unexpected problem.

Writing the article itself was never really the whole problem. I needed somewhere for ideas to start, somewhere they could develop, somewhere finished thinking could live, and a sensible way of sharing it.

For a while those things were all getting mixed together.

I had notes in Obsidian. Drafts developed through conversations. Articles on LinkedIn. Older posts elsewhere. Ideas that were clearly related to one another but were effectively trapped inside individual documents.

What I realised was that I didn’t really need a blogging platform.

I needed a publishing system.

That distinction feels familiar. When I wrote recently about the personal system I use to manage notes, tasks and ADHD, the useful part was never really the choice of tools. It was understanding the problem I was trying to solve, noticing where the friction actually was, and then giving the different parts of the system clear jobs.

I’ve ended up doing much the same thing with writing.

Separating thinking from publishing
#

Obsidian has become increasingly important to how I work.

It is where incomplete thoughts are allowed to remain incomplete.

An article might start with a sentence, an observation from a client conversation, a diagram, or something that has irritated me enough that I want to understand why.

As I develop it, I want to keep the bits that don’t eventually make the article too: alternative arguments, examples, questions, links to other ideas and things that probably deserve their own article later.

That makes Obsidian a very good knowledge base.

It doesn’t necessarily make it the thing I want to publish.

That distinction turned out to be important.

My working notes should optimise for thinking.

The published version should optimise for reading.

Trying to make one file perfectly fulfil both purposes creates some odd compromises.

A deliberate path from thought to publication
#

Once I separated those concerns, the publishing flow became much simpler.

    A[Obsidian
Thinking, research and drafts]
    B[GitHub
Public content source]
    C[Hugo
Builds the site]
    D[GitHub Pages
Publishes the site]
    E[LinkedIn
Distribution and conversation]

    A -->|article is ready| B
    B --> C
    C --> D
    D -->|selected ideas travel to| E

The point isn’t sophistication. It is creating a clear path from a working thought to a published article without making the mechanics heavier than the writing itself.

Git felt like a natural boundary
#

Once I started thinking about the published article as a separate artefact, GitHub began to make much more sense.

I’ve spent enough of my career around software engineering that source control feels like a fairly natural way of managing something that evolves.

An article has a history.

It changes.

Sometimes I make a small correction. Sometimes an argument develops substantially. Sometimes several related pieces start emerging from the same original thought.

Git gives me a simple record of that without needing to invent another content-management process.

More importantly, committing something creates a useful boundary.

There is a difference between an idea I am still developing in Obsidian and something I am prepared to put into a public repository as part of my published body of work.

That little bit of friction is actually useful.

Why Hugo?
#

I looked again at conventional blogging platforms, but most of them solve a slightly different problem.

They are very good at providing an interface for creating web content.

I already have an interface for creating content.

It is Obsidian, Markdown and increasingly the tools around my own knowledge base.

What I wanted from the website was therefore relatively modest.

Take Markdown.

Turn it into a fast, simple site.

Give the content enough structure that I can connect things over time.

Stay out of the way.

Hugo fits that model extremely well.

An article is essentially a Markdown file with some metadata around it: title, date, description, categories and some information about how it connects to the rest of the site.

The website itself can then be rebuilt from those files whenever something changes.

There isn’t really a database of articles sitting behind it. The repository is the content.

I like that.

GitHub Pages closes the loop
#

The final technical piece is GitHub Pages.

The deployment is automated through GitHub Actions. I commit the article, Hugo builds the site, and GitHub Pages publishes the result.

There are obviously many technically more sophisticated architectures I could build.

That isn’t really the point.

The goal is to make publishing cheap enough, cognitively as much as financially, that the tooling doesn’t become another reason not to write.

This is also an interesting evolution of something I was already doing several years ago. I wrote about automating Hugo deployment with GitHub Actions in 2022. The mechanics have changed since then, but the instinct is the same: automate the repetitive part and preserve attention for the part that needs thought.

Giving each platform one job
#

The more important shift was deciding that each platform had a different job.

flowchart LR
    A[Obsidian] -->|optimises for| A1[Thinking]
    B[Hugo site] -->|optimises for| B1[Reading]
    C[LinkedIn] -->|optimises for| C1[Conversation]

That sounds almost trivial written down, but it removed quite a lot of ambiguity.

Obsidian is where the idea is developed and connected to other thinking.

The Hugo site is the durable public version.

LinkedIn is useful for reach, discussion and putting an idea in front of people I already know or who are working on similar problems.

LinkedIn therefore isn’t where I want my writing to live.

It is where I want some of it to travel.

That feels healthier than treating every platform as another place that must contain another identical copy.

A small content operating model
#

This is probably why the setup feels familiar to me.

I’ve spent a lot of my career thinking about flow, architecture and operating models.

One of the recurring mistakes in all three is allowing the tools or organisational boundaries to define the process.

We buy a system and then gradually reshape the work around what the system expects.

I’m trying to do the opposite here.

Each component has a fairly explicit responsibility.

Obsidian optimises for thinking and connections.

Git provides history and a deliberate publication boundary.

Hugo turns structured content into the public site.

GitHub Pages handles delivery.

LinkedIn provides distribution and conversation.

None of those tools needs to own the entire process.

It is essentially a very small content operating model built around separation of concerns.

Building a body of thinking
#

There is another reason I’ve wanted to do this.

I don’t particularly want to build a collection of posts.

I want to build a connected body of thinking.

Some of the ideas I’m writing about now have existed in different forms throughout my career. Others are developing very quickly as AI changes what is possible in software, organisations and decision-making.

Those relationships matter.

A reader arriving because of something I’ve written about AI should eventually be able to follow the connections into enterprise context, decision-making, knowledge graphs and agency.

Someone reading about transformation might move through flow, dependencies, constraints, horizons and guardrails.

And my own working notes can contain far richer connections than I would ever expose directly on the website.

The public article becomes one expression of a much larger knowledge graph.

That is the direction I find particularly interesting.

The system will probably keep changing
#

The system I use today almost certainly isn’t the one I’ll be using in a year’s time.

That was one of the lessons from building my personal operating system too.

The objective isn’t to discover the perfect toolchain.

It is to notice the friction, understand what is causing it, and adjust the system.

Right now GitHub, Hugo and GitHub Pages give me something quite simple: ownership of the content, a durable public home, version history, an automated publishing path and enough structure to begin connecting ideas properly.

Obsidian gives me somewhere for those ideas to remain messy before they become articles.

And LinkedIn gives me somewhere to have the conversation afterwards.

The technology is useful.

But the more important change has been deciding that thinking, publishing and distribution are different activities.

Once I separated those, the technology choices became surprisingly straightforward.
