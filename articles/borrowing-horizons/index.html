Borrowing Horizons

14 September 2026·1905 words·9 mins

My seven-year-old daughter spends quite a lot of time thinking about the future, usually in the form of questions for me.

“When can I ride a motorbike?” “When can I use that power tool?” “When can I jump off that?”

Sometimes she wants a date. Is it when she’s eight? Ten? A teenager? Sometimes tomorrow seems perfectly reasonable.

What I find fascinating is the ambition behind the questions. She can imagine herself doing almost anything; what she doesn’t yet have is a particularly sophisticated understanding of the risks, dependencies or capabilities involved in getting there. Her horizon is enormous because experience hasn’t yet taught her all the reasons something might be difficult.

Working with organisations, I sometimes see almost the inverse. Experience has taught us a great deal. We know what has failed before, where the controls are, which stakeholders need to be involved and where the organisational or technical complexity lies. All of that experience is valuable, but it can also quietly shape the limits of what we believe is possible.

I’ve started to wonder how often our view of the future is really a reflection of the constraints we’ve accumulated from the past.

Where did the horizon come from?
#

I was recently looking at a process where an SLA was measured in tens of days. Within that organisation this was normal. It was planned around, measured and perhaps even considered reasonably efficient.

I’ve seen similar activities elsewhere measured in hours.

That doesn’t mean the organisation with the shorter SLA has got it right, or that its answer can simply be transplanted somewhere else. Regulation, risk, technology and the nature of the problem all matter. Comparing organisations without understanding their context isn’t particularly useful.

But knowing that somebody else operates on a radically different timescale changes the question. Instead of asking whether thirty days could become twenty-five, we can ask:

What would have to be true for this to take two hours?

I think one of the privileges of having worked across different industries, organisations and technologies is being able to borrow horizons in this way. You get to see different versions of normal. Something that appears impossibly ambitious in one organisation may be completely routine somewhere else.

I’ve found that useful because it changes the starting point for a conversation. You’re not necessarily importing somebody else’s solution or setting their performance as an arbitrary benchmark. You’re using their experience as evidence that the boundaries of the problem may be wider than the ones immediately visible to you.

Sometimes the most useful thing you can bring into a transformation isn’t an answer. It’s a different reference point.

Organisations carry their history with them
#

The next question, though, is how our existing horizon came to be where it is.

Most organisational complexity didn’t appear because somebody deliberately set out to make work difficult. Quite often there is a perfectly sensible story behind it. Something went wrong: a release failed, a customer was affected, money was lost or a regulator raised a concern. Perhaps somebody made a decision without the right information or without involving someone who needed to be involved.

We responded to the problem in front of us. We added a review, an approval, another piece of evidence or an additional control. At that moment it may have been entirely the right thing to do. Then something else happened, and we responded again.

Over time, those responses become part of how the organisation works. The people who experienced the original problem move on. Technology changes. Regulation changes. The nature of the risk may change. But the process often remains.

So perhaps nobody ever actually decided that something should take thirty days. Thirty days may simply be the accumulated consequence of a series of individually reasonable decisions made over many years.

I find that distinction important because it changes how we approach transformation. Rather than immediately asking how we reduce thirty days, perhaps we should first understand how thirty days became normal.

Which parts of that timeline manage risks that still exist? Which compensate for a limitation elsewhere? Which exist because of technology we no longer use? Which are genuinely required by regulation, and which have simply become associated with regulation through years of organisational memory?

The current process tells us how the organisation works. It doesn’t necessarily tell us how the organisation needs to work.

Borrow the horizon, not the operating model
#

There’s an idea here that I think connects with something I took from Reid Hoffman and Chris Yeh’s Blitzscaling.

The book deliberately examines an extreme environment: organisations trying to achieve very rapid scale while operating with significant uncertainty. I’m certainly not suggesting that every organisation should attempt to behave like a Silicon Valley hypergrowth company. In many of the environments I work in, doing so would be entirely inappropriate.

What I find useful is the challenge it creates to assumptions about what sensible or responsible management looks like.

Practices that are entirely rational under one set of constraints can become limiting under another. An organisation optimising for rapid growth makes different choices around speed, efficiency and uncertainty from an established organisation operating critical infrastructure. Neither operating model can simply be transplanted into the other.

But looking at the extreme is useful because it changes the reference point. It helps us distinguish between “We can’t do that” and “We have chosen not to do that under our current constraints.” Those are very different statements.

The first closes the conversation. The second invites us to understand the constraints and the choices we’ve made around them. Some will be entirely justified. Others may turn out to be assumptions that have survived longer than the circumstances that created them.

That’s an important part of what I mean by borrowing horizons. Borrow the horizon, not necessarily the operating model.

Risk and frequency
#

There is something counterintuitive here that I’ve seen repeatedly in technology. When something feels risky, our natural reaction is often to do it less frequently.

Yet one of the most useful lessons I’ve taken from DevOps is almost the opposite: if we want to become good at something, we normally need to do it more often.

That doesn’t mean repeatedly taking the same risk and hoping for a better result. We make the individual change smaller. We automate the things that can be reliably automated. We improve observability, shorten the feedback loop and make it easier to recover when something goes wrong.

The result is that something which used to be an event gradually becomes routine.

I’ve seen releases that once required significant planning, coordination and a room full of people become something teams do many times a day. The underlying need for control hasn’t disappeared. Instead, we’ve changed how that control is achieved and built capability through repetition.

That idea is useful well beyond software delivery. When we do something rarely, knowledge decays between each occurrence and the activity remains exceptional. When we can safely do it more frequently, we create more opportunities to learn, improve and eventually automate parts of it.

This is one reason I think ambition needs more than a bold target. If somebody tells a team accustomed to thirty days that the new target is two hours, it is quite reasonable for them to be sceptical. Their experience tells them something very different.

Borrowing a horizon tells us that a different outcome may be possible. The work of transformation is understanding what needs to change to make that outcome credible.

Looking at the system differently
#

This is also why I continue to find techniques such as value stream mapping useful. The value isn’t simply in drawing the current process or finding the box with the largest number written beside it. A different horizon gives us a reason to interrogate the system more deeply.

If something takes twelve days, what actually happens during those twelve days? How much of it is work and how much is waiting? Where is judgement genuinely required? Where are we managing risk? Where are we compensating for another constraint in the system? And, perhaps most importantly, which of those things would we design differently if we were starting today?

That last question is becoming increasingly interesting because the capabilities available to us are changing quickly.

AI is an obvious example. There is understandably a lot of interest in identifying activities where AI can improve productivity, but starting with the existing activity can also constrain our thinking.

We might use an LLM to produce in thirty seconds a document that previously took someone three hours to prepare. That’s useful. But if the document then waits four days for somebody to review it, we should probably be curious about the four days as well.

There is also a danger that we assume AI itself must be the answer to each of those questions. It may not be. Sometimes deterministic automation, a policy engine or an existing tool will provide a more predictable answer. In other cases, once we understand why an activity exists, removing it entirely may create more value than automating it.

The important change is that our available capabilities have moved. AI can work with context and information in ways that were difficult to achieve previously. Automation can remove repeatable activity. Modern platforms can make capabilities available on demand that once took weeks to provision. The assumptions that shaped a process five or ten years ago therefore deserve to be revisited.

So the question isn’t simply “Where can we use AI?” or even “What can we automate?”

It’s whether those new capabilities should change what we believe good could look like in the first place.

Making a different future credible
#

This is where I think borrowing horizons becomes more than benchmarking.

A benchmark tells us how somebody else performs. A horizon helps us imagine a different version of our own organisation.

We can look across industries, at organisations operating at a different scale, at new technologies or simply at teams that have approached the same problem differently. None of those gives us an answer we can copy without thought. What they give us is evidence that the assumptions embedded in our current system aren’t the only assumptions available.

From there, we can work backwards. What prevents that outcome today? Which constraints are fundamental and which have accumulated? What capabilities are missing? Which risks need to be managed differently? Where could shorter feedback loops allow us to learn rather than trying to design the entire future upfront?

That feels more useful to me than simply telling organisations to be more ambitious. Ambition without an understanding of risk is easy. The more interesting leadership challenge is retaining the ability to imagine something radically different after you’ve learned how complicated the world really is.

Perhaps that’s something I want my daughter to retain too.

As she gets older, she’ll inevitably develop a better understanding of why she can’t do everything tomorrow. That’s a good thing. But I hope that understanding risk doesn’t stop her looking at something new and asking, “When can I do that?”

Organisations could probably afford to ask that question a little more often too. Not because somebody else doing something means we should simply copy them, but because their experience gives us another horizon against which to test our own assumptions.

If that is possible somewhere else, what would have to be true for it to become normal here?
