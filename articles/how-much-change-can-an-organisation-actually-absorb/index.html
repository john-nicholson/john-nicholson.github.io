How Much Change Can an Organisation Actually Absorb?

23 September 2026·1932 words·10 mins

There is a lot of excitement around AI-enabled modernisation, and with good reason. Analysis can be accelerated, code can be generated or transformed faster, testing can be improved, and large estates that once looked prohibitively expensive to tackle can begin to look much more manageable. But that creates a new problem: if the technology can move faster, how much change can the organisation actually absorb?

That question is rarely asked clearly enough at the beginning of a modernisation programme. We tend to model engineering capacity, delivery waves, migration throughput, environments and technical dependencies in some detail, but we do not always model with the same discipline the capacity of the organisation itself to consume the change being produced. That matters because transformation is not complete when the new capability exists. It is complete when the organisation has actually moved.

This follows directly from the problem I explored in Scaling AI Modernisation Means Scaling Decisions, Not Just Code: once we start treating modernisation as a flow rather than a code-generation problem, organisational capacity becomes another constraint in that flow.

AI changes expectations as well as delivery
#

There is another dynamic emerging around this. Clients increasingly expect AI-enabled programmes to be quicker, cheaper and better. That expectation is understandable because the wider narrative around AI strongly suggests that delivery should accelerate, cost should fall and quality should improve at the same time.

Sometimes that expectation is justified, but it can also create a significant expectation differential between what AI can accelerate technically and what the wider organisation can absorb operationally. A supplier may genuinely be able to analyse an application estate faster, generate transformations more quickly, automate more testing and reduce the effort needed for repetitive engineering work. None of that automatically makes business validation, regulatory approval, user adoption, operational readiness or organisational decision-making move at the same speed.

The danger is that the client hears “AI makes modernisation faster” and reasonably concludes that the whole programme should compress in proportion. That is where tension starts to appear, because the engineering portion of the work may shrink significantly while many of the non-engineering constraints remain largely unchanged. If those constraints are not made visible early, the programme can appear slower than expected even while the technology component is performing extremely well.

AI can therefore improve the capability of one part of the system while making the limits of the rest of the system much more obvious.

Transformation has an absorption limit
#

Most organisations have a finite ability to deal with concurrent change. People still have day jobs. Operational teams still need to run services. Managers still need to support teams. Front-line workers still need to learn new processes, subject-matter experts still need to validate decisions, and governance functions still need to review changes.

There is a point beyond which more change does not mean more progress. It simply creates more work in progress.

This is particularly important where modernisation affects how people work day to day. A platform change may look technical from the programme perspective while changing access patterns, controls, approvals, reporting, support routes or operational procedures for the people using it. Those impacts accumulate, and an organisation may be perfectly capable of absorbing one or two significant changes in a period but not ten.

The technical programme may have capacity for ten. The organisation may not. That difference becomes a constraint on the whole system.

The field-of-dreams problem
#

This is where some IT-led transformations start to become “build it and they will come” programmes. The new platform is built, the new process is available, the migration is technically possible, and the assumption is that adoption will naturally follow.

Sometimes it does. Often only part of the organisation moves.

That can leave the organisation in a worse position than before because the new platform now has to be operated, but the old platform cannot be retired. Some users follow the new process while others remain on the old one. Support teams have to deal with both, controls may exist in two places, reporting becomes fragmented, and the original risk or cost the programme was meant to remove still exists because the old estate has not actually disappeared.

The transformation has delivered change, but not completion. That is why one of the most useful measures of transformation is not simply whether the new capability has gone live, but whether the old capability can be turned off.

A transformation is not complete when the new capability exists. It is complete when the old way of working is no longer required.

That is a much harder standard, but a much more meaningful one.

Change capacity is not constant
#

Another mistake we make is assuming that organisational capacity is broadly stable over time. It is not.

Most organisations have predictable periods when their ability to absorb change drops significantly. Common examples include:

  • regulatory freezes that limit production change;
  • financial year-end, when finance, operations and leadership teams are heavily constrained;
  • peak trading periods where operational stability takes precedence;
  • major audits, reporting cycles or annual planning windows;
  • business-critical periods where certain changes are simply too disruptive.

These are not exceptional events. They are part of the operating environment.

If we were modelling an engineering system, we would treat this as capacity variation. Yet transformation programmes often build plans that assume the organisation is equally available throughout the year and then describe the resulting delays as stakeholder issues.

A more useful model would make those constraints explicit.

    A[Engineering Capacity] --> C[Transformation Throughput]
    B[Business Change Capacity] --> C
    D[Environment Capacity] --> C
    E[Decision / SME Capacity] --> C

    F[Year End / Regulatory Freeze / Peak Period]
    F -. reduces .-> B
    F -. reduces .-> D
    F -. reduces .-> E

The throughput of the transformation is constrained by the lowest available capacity across the system, not simply by the speed at which engineering work can be produced.

Shorter programmes make these constraints more important
#

This becomes particularly significant in an AI-enabled modernisation programme because one of the promises is that the programme itself becomes shorter. If AI allows us to analyse and transform an estate in months rather than years, then a six-week regulatory freeze or an unavailable business-validation team becomes proportionally much more significant.

A pause that represented a small percentage of a multi-year programme can become a major part of a compressed delivery window. That creates a practical challenge for suppliers as well: if the engineering team can continue working but the client cannot validate, approve or provide subject-matter expertise, what should the delivery organisation do during that period?

The traditional answer is often to keep producing:

  • continue analysing applications;
  • continue generating code;
  • continue preparing migrations;
  • build a backlog ready for when the client becomes available again.

But that can create exactly the WIP problem we should be trying to avoid. The supplier looks productive because work continues to be generated, but the programme accumulates a growing queue of unvalidated change. When the client becomes available again, they are no longer faced with one decision or one migration; they are faced with dozens.

That does not improve flow. It simply moves the bottleneck.

Keep the team valuable, not merely busy
#

This changes the supplier question from “How do we keep the team busy while the client is unavailable?” to “What work increases future throughput while the primary flow is constrained?”

A regulatory freeze may prevent deployment but not estate discovery. Business SMEs may be unavailable for validation while dependency information is improved. Production change may be restricted while automated testing, migration archetypes, code semantics, observability or environment readiness are strengthened.

The important distinction is between work that creates more inventory and work that improves the system’s ability to process future change. Generating another hundred unvalidated migrations may simply increase WIP, while improving test automation, dependency confidence or the semantic model of the estate may increase the speed at which those migrations can safely move once the constraint is removed.

That is particularly important in outsourced delivery, where commercial pressure can easily reward visible activity rather than genuine system improvement.

Change should be modelled like any other dependency
#

We already accept that technical systems have dependency chains. Application B may need to move before application C, a shared service may need to be upgraded before a downstream application can transition, or a test environment may need to be available before a release can proceed.

Organisations have dependency chains too. A team may need training before a process changes. A manager may need access to new reporting before an old control can be removed. A service desk may need new support capability before a platform goes live. A policy may need to change before behaviour can safely follow.

These are not just communications tasks. They are dependencies in the transformation flow.

Once we treat them that way, the programme becomes much easier to reason about. The question becomes: what has to be true before this change can safely move to the next state?

That is exactly the same reasoning we apply to technical modernisation.

Calendar constraints can become part of the model
#

There is an interesting AI implication here as well. If a modernisation system already understands dependencies, migration archetypes, environments and decision rights, there is no reason it cannot also understand organisational calendars and operating constraints.

It can know that:

  • a particular application group cannot deploy during a regulatory freeze;
  • finance SMEs have sharply reduced availability around year-end;
  • a critical operational function has a restricted change window;
  • a particular environment is unavailable during another programme’s testing cycle.

Those constraints can be treated as part of the migration route in exactly the same way as a technical dependency. That means the system can sequence work around the organisation rather than discovering those constraints after the work has already been prepared.

This is an important evolution of the idea of enterprise context. Enterprise context is not just technical context; it also includes how the organisation actually operates.

Faster delivery can expose slower adoption
#

There is a broader consequence here. AI may dramatically increase our capacity to produce change, but if organisational absorption capacity does not increase at the same rate, then the bottleneck simply moves.

We may end up with technically complete capabilities waiting for:

  • training;
  • validation;
  • policy changes;
  • operational readiness;
  • business adoption.

That is effectively the same queueing problem as code waiting for a test environment. The difference is that the queue is now made up of people, decisions and operating processes.

This is why the client expectation of “faster, cheaper, better” needs to be handled carefully. The promise should not simply be that every part of the programme becomes faster because AI is present. The better promise is that AI allows us to compress the parts of the flow that can genuinely be accelerated, while giving us better visibility of the constraints that cannot.

That creates a more realistic and useful conversation about where time and cost can actually be removed.

The real measure is whether the old world disappears
#

The organisations that get this right will not simply become better at delivering change. They will become better at understanding how much change they can absorb, where that capacity sits, how it varies over time and how to sequence transformation around it.

That matters because building the future state is only half the job. The value appears when the organisation can leave the old state behind.

AI may significantly increase our capacity to produce change. That makes understanding the organisation’s capacity to consume change more important, not less.
