The Real Power of Agentic Systems Is Closing the Loop

22 September 2026·1353 words·7 mins

There is a lot being written about AI agents at the moment, and much of the conversation understandably focuses on autonomy: agents that can execute tasks, call tools, communicate with other systems and increasingly operate across whole workflows. I think that risks missing one of the more interesting opportunities, because the real power of agentic systems is not simply that they can act. It is that they can close the loop between information, ownership, decisions and change.

That distinction matters because most organisations already have a significant amount of automation. Workflow engines, APIs, rules engines, integration platforms and orchestration tooling can execute known processes quickly, reliably and repeatedly. AI does not make those capabilities obsolete; in many cases, combining them with AI is considerably more powerful than trying to replace them. A system might use deterministic automation for the things we understand clearly, enterprise knowledge to provide context, an AI reasoning layer to interpret circumstances, and memory to understand how those circumstances have changed over time. Agency then adds something different: the ability to recognise that an intervention is required and actively pursue it.

From knowledge repositories to living organisational context
#

Consider something relatively mundane: an information-security governance control. Most organisations have policies, standards, regulatory interpretations, architecture decisions and operating procedures spread across numerous repositories. Traditionally, we have attempted to solve this by creating a better library. More recently, we have started putting AI over that library so people can ask questions of it. Both are useful, but neither necessarily solves the underlying problem.

Imagine a new document enters the organisation and appears to contradict the currently accepted interpretation of a regulatory requirement. One response is to ignore it until somebody notices. Another is to allow only explicitly curated information into the enterprise knowledge model. That protects quality, but creates a different weakness: the organisation’s understanding is only as current as its manual curation process.

There is another possibility. The system can recognise the contradiction and, because the enterprise context contains not just information but also ownership, identify which person or role is accountable for that area. It can compare the new information with the current organisational understanding, summarise the delta, assemble the relevant evidence and put the issue directly in front of the knowledge owner.

That interaction might happen through a governance portal for something formal, through email where an audit trail is important, or through Teams or Slack where a lighter interaction is sufficient. The communication channel is not the interesting part. What matters is that the system can understand what has changed, what existing knowledge might be affected, who owns that knowledge, what decision is required and what should happen once that decision has been made. That starts to look considerably more powerful than document management or even enterprise search.

Knowledge needs to be maintained, not merely retrieved
#

This is one of the reasons I think enterprise knowledge graphs and ontologies become increasingly important as organisations move further into AI. It is not enough to hydrate a graph once and treat it as a static representation of the organisation because businesses change continuously: people move roles, regulations evolve, contracts change, markets move and even the interpretation of existing information changes over time.

If AI systems are going to reason against enterprise context, the quality and currency of that context becomes a form of critical infrastructure. That suggests an interesting class of agent: agents whose role is to maintain the context used by other agents and systems. They do not necessarily make business decisions themselves; instead, they can continually identify inconsistencies, stale knowledge, missing ownership, conflicting evidence or changes in the underlying sources from which the organisation’s understanding has been derived.

Where confidence is high and policy permits it, some changes might be applied automatically. Where judgement or accountability is required, the system can escalate to the appropriate owner with the relevant context already assembled. This is also where the combination of deterministic systems and AI becomes important. I would not want an AI model arbitrarily rewriting an organisation’s governance position because it has discovered a new PDF, but I might be very comfortable with an AI system identifying the potential contradiction, locating the accountable owner, explaining the delta and asking for a structured decision. Once that decision is made, deterministic automation can apply it consistently.

Agency does not remove accountability
#

There is an important architectural and organisational principle underneath this: agency should not mean removing humans from a system. It should mean being much more deliberate about where humans are actually required. There are activities where AI can act autonomously, many where deterministic automation remains the better answer, others where AI can prepare a decision but a human should retain authority, and still more where organisations decide that AI should not participate at all.

The more interesting design question therefore becomes less “Can an agent do this?” and more “Where should the decision right sit, and what information does each actor need in order to exercise it well?” That is not purely a technology question; it is an operating-model question as well. It also builds on the distinction between human presence and genuine human judgement I explored in Human in the loop isn’t the same as human judgement.

It also connects directly to the wider conversation about controls and guardrails. The objective should not be to insert a human into every step simply because doing so feels safer, nor should we assume that removing humans creates a better system. The aim is to place judgement where judgement has value, deterministic controls where the answer is known, and agency where the system benefits from being able to recognise and pursue change.

Compressing organisational latency
#

The business value becomes clearer when you think about all of this in terms of latency. In a traditional organisation, somebody may discover new information, realise that it conflicts with an existing policy, find the right team, identify the appropriate SME, arrange a conversation, explain the context and eventually trigger a change. Depending on the organisation, that can take days, weeks or occasionally months.

An intelligent system can compress much of that journey, not because it necessarily makes the final decision, but because it can recognise the event, identify the relevant owner, assemble the context and put the decision in front of them within minutes. For regulatory policy, that degree of speed may not always matter. For other areas it can matter a great deal: a shift in market conditions, a new commercial opportunity, a changing supply constraint, a new contract structure or a change in a customer’s risk profile can all create situations where the value lies partly in how quickly the organisation recognises the change and responds.

The same pattern applies in each case: detect the change, understand it in the context of the enterprise, identify ownership, determine whether existing rules still apply, engage the appropriate decision-maker where required, update the enterprise context and allow the systems operating against that context to adapt.

From automation to an adaptive enterprise
#

This is why I think some of the most powerful applications of agentic AI will look less like isolated assistants and more like an organisational nervous system. The deterministic systems remain, as do the APIs and workflows, but around them we can create a contextual layer capable of understanding the enterprise, recognising change and directing attention towards the places where decisions need to be made.

Memory allows that system to understand how its context has evolved over time. Knowledge graphs make relationships and ownership explicit. Reasoning helps interpret ambiguity. Agents provide the ability to act, while deterministic automation gives us reliability where the required behaviour is already understood. None of those components is particularly revolutionary in isolation; the interesting part is what happens when they are combined.

The organisations that get this right may find that the biggest benefit of AI is not simply doing today’s work faster. It is reducing the time between the world changing and the organisation understanding what it needs to do about it. That, to me, is a much more interesting definition of an agentic enterprise.
