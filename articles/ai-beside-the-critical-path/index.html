AI Beside the Critical Path

19 September 2026·1513 words·8 mins

I had a conversation with a client recently that crystallised something I have been thinking about for a while.

Like many organisations, they are asking reasonable questions about the value they are getting from AI. There has been significant experimentation, plenty of interest and no shortage of potential use cases, but there is still a sense that the value being realised does not quite match the scale of the conversation around it.

What struck me was how AI was being discussed. AI was one thing. Digital transformation was another. Service transformation sat somewhere else again.

I increasingly think those two things are connected, because if we continue to treat AI as something additional, sitting beside the systems and services through which value is actually delivered, we should probably not be surprised when the value it creates is incremental too.

AI beside the critical path
#

A lot of current enterprise AI adoption sits beside the critical path. It helps somebody draft an email, summarise a document, search for information, prepare a response or create the first version of something that a person would otherwise have produced manually.

Those things can be useful. Saving time repeatedly across a large workforce is not meaningless, and improving access to information can remove genuine friction. But in many cases the service itself has not changed.

The customer still makes the same request. It still enters the same queue. It still moves through the same teams, approvals and hand-offs. The same systems are updated and the same organisational boundaries determine how quickly the work progresses.

We have made somebody standing beside the process faster without necessarily making the process itself flow any better. That matters because the value we expect from AI is often much larger than the intervention we have actually made.

We talk about transformation while deploying augmentation.

Perhaps we do not have an AI ROI problem
#

Imagine a service where a request spends two days waiting for approval.

Someone has introduced AI to help prepare the approval pack. What previously took ten minutes can now be done in thirty seconds. That is an improvement, but the request still waits two days.

If we stop there, the AI has made an activity faster without changing the outcome that matters to the customer.

The more interesting question is why that approval takes two days in the first place. Perhaps somebody has to gather information from several systems, understand the request, compare it against policy, assess the associated risk and decide whether an exception is acceptable.

Now the design space starts to change. Instead of only asking how to accelerate the existing activity, we can ask:

  • Could the relevant context be assembled automatically?
  • Could explicit policy determine the obvious cases?
  • Could existing deterministic automation carry out the predictable actions?
  • Could AI identify ambiguity, explain why a case is unusual and assemble the evidence needed for a decision?
  • Could only the genuinely exceptional cases reach a person?

At that point we are no longer asking how AI can help someone perform the approval more quickly. We are asking whether the approval should exist in its current form at all.

That is a much more significant intervention.

Perhaps some of the disappointing return from AI is entirely rational. If we save a knowledge worker fifteen minutes on an activity that represents only a small part of the lead time of a service, there is a limit to the enterprise value we should expect from that saving.

Even multiplied across thousands of people, local productivity gains do not automatically change the economics of the service. In a constrained system they may simply create spare capacity on one side of the constraint.

We may not have an AI ROI problem. We may have an AI placement problem.

Redesigning the path
#

Putting AI onto the critical path does not mean handing the organisation over to an LLM. That is too simplistic, and probably one reason these conversations become unnecessarily polarised.

The more interesting architecture combines different forms of capability. Some decisions are better expressed as deterministic rules. Some actions should be carried out by existing workflow or automation platforms. Some situations require human judgement. AI can help interpret demand, assemble context, deal with ambiguity and decide which of those capabilities should be invoked.

A service might begin with a customer request. AI helps interpret what is being asked and gathers the relevant enterprise context. Explicit policy handles cases where the organisation already knows the correct answer. Deterministic automation performs repeatable actions. AI helps reason across less structured situations, and a person becomes involved where judgement, accountability or genuine exception handling is required.

The point is not that AI replaces the service. The point is that the existence of these capabilities allows us to reconsider the design of the service.

The progression is therefore not simply:

manual work → AI work

It is closer to:

understand the flow → identify the constraints and decisions → redesign the service using the right combination of AI, automation, policy and human judgement

Once you look at it that way, the AI use case becomes almost secondary.

The service is the thing being transformed.

Adoption is not integration
#

An organisation can have thousands of licensed users, dozens of proofs of concept and an impressive portfolio of AI initiatives while barely changing how value moves through the enterprise. That is because adoption and integration are different things.

Adoption is relatively easy to measure. We can count users, prompts, copilots, experiments and use cases. But those measures tell us very little about whether the organisation itself is behaving differently.

If the objective is service improvement, the questions should look much more familiar:

  • Has lead time changed?
  • Has a queue disappeared?
  • Can a request be resolved at first contact?
  • Have hand-offs reduced?
  • Can the organisation handle more demand without simply adding more people?
  • Can something that previously took days now happen in hours?

Those are not particularly novel AI metrics. They are measures of whether the system improved.

And perhaps that is the point.

The organisational problem
#

There is another consequence of treating AI as a separate thing.

If the CIO has an AI programme optimising employee activities, Digital is redesigning customer journeys, Service is working on operational improvement and the business is pursuing its own transformation agenda, who owns the redesign of the complete value stream?

Dedicated AI capability makes sense, particularly while organisations establish skills, platforms, controls and patterns. But there is a danger if AI remains permanently separated from service design, architecture, digital and operating-model transformation, because its organisational placement may start to shape the opportunities it can see.

If an AI team is asked to find AI use cases, it will find AI use cases. If a service team is asked to improve flow, it may discover that AI is part of the answer alongside policy changes, automation, architecture changes or removing work entirely.

Those are very different starting points.

The question shifts from “Where can we use AI?” to “How should this service work now that these capabilities exist?”

That opens a much larger design space.

Being on the critical path raises the standard
#

There is a good reason organisations have been cautious about moving AI closer to critical services.

Once it becomes part of the path through which value is delivered, reliability matters differently. Context matters. Observability matters. Failure modes matter. Security, cost and accountability matter. You need to understand when the system should reason, when it should follow an explicit rule and when it should stop and involve a person.

Those are real concerns, but they are also recognisable ones. We already know how to think about critical systems through architecture, engineering, operational resilience and service design. AI introduces new behaviours and new risks, but it does not remove the need to understand the system as a whole.

In some ways, moving AI towards the critical path forces a healthier conversation. It moves us from asking whether somebody saved a few minutes writing something to asking whether we trust the service we have designed.

Look at where the AI actually sits
#

None of this makes productivity tools a mistake. Helping people work more effectively has real value, and for many organisations it is a sensible place to begin.

The problem comes when we expect transformational economics from an intervention that sits outside the part of the system determining the outcome. If removing the AI would leave the service flow essentially unchanged, then we should probably be realistic about the kind of transformation we have delivered.

A useful exercise is to take one important service and draw the path from demand to outcome. Then mark:

  • the queues;
  • the hand-offs;
  • the decisions;
  • the constraints;
  • and where the AI investments actually sit.

If most of them are beside that path, disappointing returns may not be particularly surprising.

The opportunity is not simply to put AI on the critical path. It is to use AI, automation and human judgement to ask whether the critical path should still look like that at all.
