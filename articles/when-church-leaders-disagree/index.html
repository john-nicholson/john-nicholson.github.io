When Church Leaders Disagree

19 October 2026·1654 words·8 mins
When Good Arrangements Become Traps - This article is part of a series.
Part : This Article

I think a good governance meeting needs different opinions. If everybody brings the same perspective, it becomes difficult to know whether we are holding one another accountable or simply confirming what we already think. And, frankly, it gets boring.

In Death by Meeting, Patrick Lencioni makes a useful connection between meetings and films. We will willingly give our attention to a story in which something matters, different possibilities compete and we want to know how it will unfold. Yet we can sit through meetings where the outcome feels settled before the discussion begins. The point is not that governance needs manufactured drama. It is that there ought to be something worth thinking about together, and a discussion that takes us somewhere.

His work on The Five Dysfunctions of a Team brings another part of this into focus: fear of conflict can preserve artificial harmony at the expense of productive debate. In church leadership, we can be so concerned to preserve unity that we become uncomfortable with differences of opinion. But agreement is not, by itself, evidence of unity, any more than disagreement is necessarily evidence that a relationship has broken down.

That does not mean every difficult meeting is a healthy one. Different perspectives can improve a decision; confused responsibilities can leave people arguing without knowing whose decision it is. Learning to distinguish those two things has been an important part of my experience as a church trustee.

Different responsibilities under pressure
#

I became a trustee during the second lockdown, not long after leaving the company I had founded. I wasn’t completely new to governance. I had served as a parent governor, sat as a board director and seen governance operate across the colleges I was supporting. I understood, at least intellectually, why the distinction between governance and management mattered.

But I was also coming directly out of founder mode. For years, if something needed doing, the instinct was to step into the gap. Responsibility, authority and delivery often sat close together because that was what the organisation required. Becoming a trustee meant learning when that instinct was useful and when it risked getting in somebody else’s way.

Covid made that particularly difficult. Rules affecting churches were changing rapidly, the consequences of getting them wrong mattered, and there was no obvious expert sitting somewhere with the definitive interpretation. Decisions sometimes had to be made against guidance that might change again the following week.

What I remember most clearly is that nobody came away from those trustee meetings happy.

The church leadership were trying to lead people through an extraordinary situation, but often felt that their hands were tied by questions of compliance, risk and what we could responsibly do. Trustees carried the legal responsibility for the charity and were understandably cautious. The employed team were trying to translate all of that into something workable: services moving online, pastoral care changing shape, buildings opening and closing, and ordinary ways of working disappearing almost overnight.

Nobody was being unreasonable. Each group was carrying responsibilities that mattered, and under pressure those responsibilities pulled in different directions. The difficulty came when those differences began to feel like opposition.

When helping becomes part of the problem
#

As the church began to open up again, two problems needed attention.

One was the amount of operational work resting on a few trustees. We had stepped into gaps because work needed doing, but arrangements formed in an emergency do not necessarily unwind when the emergency ends. Trusteeship had become more demanding than it needed to be, and harder to recruit people into. We were asking people to take responsibility for governance while also carrying a substantial part of the work being governed.

The other problem was the relationship between trustees and elders. It had become fractious, and the boundary between scrutinising direction and setting it was not clear enough. Trustees needed to ask whether the church’s decisions were faithful to its purpose, responsible and sustainable. But that could drift into deciding the direction ourselves.

Those were related problems, but they were not the same problem. Moving some operational work away from trustees would not, by itself, settle the question of authority. Equally, a clearer description of responsibilities would not create the capacity needed to carry the work.

There was no single meeting at which we resolved all of this. Changes in both teams helped the culture change, alongside a concerted effort to clarify where trustee boundaries needed to be. It was a gradual process rather than a clean organisational reset.

Nor was the answer to reduce trusteeship to checking compliance. Trustees remain responsible for the charity pursuing its purposes and stewarding its people and resources well. That requires judgement about direction, not just reassurance that the paperwork is in order. But it does not follow that trustees should originate every proposal, make every operational choice or become an alternative leadership team.

For our church, the task was to make the relationship between trustees and elders clearer without making either role less substantial. Churches allocate these responsibilities differently, according to their governing documents and church structures; our experience is not a prescription for every church. We needed room for challenge without every challenge reopening the question of who was leading.

Enough trust to disagree
#

This is where I find Lencioni’s connection between trust and conflict useful. It is tempting to think that a team which trusts one another will disagree less. But trust should make it easier to say, “I don’t think we’ve considered this properly,” or, “I’m not convinced this is the right thing to do.” We should be able to question a proposal without the person bringing it having to defend their commitment to the church.

That matters because a proposal can be well intentioned, carefully prepared and still need challenging. Somebody may see a consequence that others have missed. Somebody closer to the work may know that the capacity simply is not there. Another person may recognise that an arrangement which works through trusted personal relationships will become much harder to sustain when those people move on.

If we move too quickly to agreement, we lose the benefit of having those people in the room. Their presence matters less if the only acceptable contribution is support.

One tempting response to difficult relationships is to make the circle smaller: work with people who already understand one another and rely on shared history to get things done. There are good reasons why churches value those relationships. Familiarity can help people speak plainly and interpret one another generously. The risk comes when it makes challenge less likely rather than more possible.

We may stop asking a question because we already know who will find it uncomfortable. We may assume that somebody else has examined a proposal because we trust the person bringing it. Or we may treat the absence of disagreement as evidence that the arrangements are working.

The Charity Commission’s guidance for charities in England and Wales makes this responsibility explicit. In The essential trustee, trustees are expected to critically and objectively review proposals and challenge assumptions, rather than simply defer to dominant personalities. Knowing somebody’s character should help us have a difficult conversation, not become a reason why the conversation is unnecessary.

Making room for challenge
#

Looking back, I do not think the task was simply to make our meetings better. We needed to change how responsibility was carried and how authority was exercised. Too much operational work had gathered around a few trustees, while the relationship between trustees and elders had become more difficult than it needed to be. A more open conversation could help us recognise those problems, but conversation alone would not resolve them.

Trust should allow us to question one another honestly. We also need clarity about what we are responsible for, what we have delegated and where our authority ends. Otherwise, even a well-intentioned challenge can become another way of taking over.

For trustees, that means being willing to examine decisions without assuming that our preferred approach should prevail. It also means retaining enough independence of judgement to recognise when something needs challenging, even when it comes from people we know well and respect. Neither familiarity nor confidence in someone’s character removes our responsibility to think for ourselves.

Elders need room to exercise the leadership entrusted to them under the church’s own arrangements. That is a distinct question from the operational decisions trustees may delegate to staff, volunteers or committees. Those carrying delegated responsibilities also need genuine room to act. If trustees remain involved in every significant operational choice, delegation has not achieved very much. But stepping back from those choices requires arrangements through which trustees can understand what is happening, ask substantive questions and act when necessary. The Commission’s decision-making guidance is useful here: where trustees delegate decisions, they need clear decision-making powers, reporting and accountability, while the board remains responsible for delegated decisions.

The change we needed was therefore more substantial than learning to disagree politely. Changes in people helped, as did deliberate work on boundaries.

I have become less convinced that healthy governance should feel frictionless. Some tension reveals confusion that needs addressing. Some comes from people bringing different experience and judgement to decisions that deserve scrutiny. The task is to distinguish between them, rather than trying to remove the discomfort from both.

The same is true of the arrangements themselves. People step into gaps, responsibilities accumulate and trusted relationships make things possible. Those arrangements may serve the church well for a season. The harder question is whether we can recognise when they have begun to limit the people and purpose they were meant to serve.

This is the starting point for a series I am calling When Good Arrangements Become Traps: how initially useful arrangements can concentrate authority, weaken challenge or leave too much depending on too few people, and what movement towards healthier practice might look like.
