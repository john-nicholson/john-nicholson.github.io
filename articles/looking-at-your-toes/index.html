If You Keep Looking at Your Toes, You’ll Never See the Horizon

15 September 2026·1756 words·9 mins

I have been working with a couple of teams recently where, despite the problems being quite different, I keep seeing the same behaviour.

They are looking at their toes.

In one case, it is a technical team dealing with a standard security-related change process. There is a substantial amount of work in flight, different kinds of requests are mixed together, and the team is struggling to see what is actually happening across the system. Work enters, progresses through a series of activities and approvals, gets delayed, is picked up again, and eventually comes out the other side. Everyone is busy, but it is remarkably difficult to answer some fairly basic questions: what is holding things up, which work is genuinely complex, where is the constraint, and what could we change to make the whole thing flow better?

The image that keeps coming to mind is an old manufacturing floor where work-in-progress has been allowed to accumulate between workstations. Eventually there is so much inventory piled up that you cannot even see the next workstation. The work itself obscures the system.

That feels very similar to what happens in overloaded technology teams. When there are enough tickets, escalations, meetings, exceptions and urgent requests in front of you, your field of vision gradually gets shorter. You stop looking at how the system works because almost all of your energy is consumed by moving the next thing. Strangely, the harder everyone works, the harder it can become to see how to make the work easier.

Looking down can be a perfectly rational response
#

The second example looks quite different. I am working with an organisation dealing with a highly regulated, manually intensive process. Some of the decisions involved have significant contractual consequences and can affect investments worth millions of pounds. There has previously been considerable scrutiny around how parts of the process were handled, so accuracy, control and evidence understandably matter.

The reaction has been to become extremely careful, and that reaction makes sense. If you have tripped over, the natural response is to watch where you put your feet next. If an organisation has made mistakes, particularly visible or expensive ones, increased scrutiny is not irrational. People check more things. Decisions are escalated. Evidence is gathered. Processes become tighter.

The problem comes when a short-term protective behaviour becomes the long-term operating model.

Looking at your toes is a great way to make sure you do not trip over the thing immediately in front of you. But if you continue looking at your toes, you will never see where you are going. You certainly will not run.

There is something interesting in that analogy. As children learn to move confidently, they gradually stop concentrating entirely on every individual step. Running requires us to look further ahead. We are constantly processing the terrain in front of us, but our attention has moved towards where we are trying to get to. Organisations need to do something similar. That does not mean ignoring immediate risks. It means becoming capable enough at dealing with the immediate terrain that we regain the ability to look towards the horizon.

Stress shortens the horizon
#

I have written before about the importance of borrowing horizons: about asking not simply how we make today’s process slightly better, but what might become possible if we reconsider some of its assumptions. There is another side to that idea.

Sometimes teams do not lack ambition. They lack the capacity required to exercise it.

When an organisation is under sustained pressure, its planning horizon contracts. The immediate problem becomes dominant. The overdue request, the next regulatory response, today’s incident, the thing somebody senior has just escalated — these consume attention because there is rarely enough capacity remaining to think about what would stop the same situation happening again.

That is why telling an overloaded team to “be more strategic” is often fairly useless advice. Strategy requires some distance from the immediate problem. You need enough space to observe patterns, challenge assumptions and imagine alternatives. You need time to ask why ten apparently different problems keep occurring at the same point in the process. You need enough cognitive room to notice that something everyone accepts as inevitable may simply be the consequence of how the system has been designed. If every available moment is already consumed by execution, that capacity disappears.

Busyness has a nonlinear cost
#

This is one reason Tom DeMarco’s Slack has stayed with me. One of its central challenges is to the idea that maximum efficiency makes an organisation more responsive. In reality, removing all spare capacity can have precisely the opposite effect.

Queueing theory gives us a useful way of understanding why. As utilisation rises, waiting time does not rise neatly alongside it. As a resource approaches full utilisation, relatively small increases in demand can produce disproportionately large increases in queues and response times.

At 50% utilisation, there is usually capacity available to absorb the next piece of work. At 80 or 90%, the probability that new work arrives while existing work is still being handled becomes much greater. Push towards 100% and almost every piece of incoming work has to wait behind something else.

This is an abstraction, of course. People are not servers and technology teams are not mathematically tidy queues. But the behaviour is recognisable. It explains the team where everyone appears incredibly busy but nothing seems to move quickly. It explains why something genuinely urgent creates chaos rather than simply being absorbed. And it explains why overloaded teams struggle so much with improvement: the spare capacity we sometimes dismiss as inefficiency is often precisely the capacity that would allow somebody to understand why the system keeps creating the work in the first place.

Slack is not necessarily unused capacity. Sometimes it is thinking capacity.

The horizon cannot belong only to the leader
#

There is an obvious counterargument here: surely looking towards the horizon is the leader’s job. To an extent, yes. Leadership should provide direction, create context and help people understand where they are trying to go. There is also a difference between leadership and management, although that is probably a different article.

But I do not think the future view should exist only at the top of an organisation. Some of the best observations about how a system could work differently come from the people closest to it. They see the repeated exception. They know which approval adds little value. They notice that three requests which look identical on a dashboard require completely different levels of effort. They know which workaround everybody quietly performs because the formal process does not quite work.

These people may not carry the title of leader, but they influence how teams think and how systems evolve. The danger is that persistent overload suppresses those voices. If somebody is permanently trying to catch up, there is little opportunity to develop an idea about how the process might change. If an organisation has created an environment where making a mistake is more visible than improving the system, people will naturally optimise for not being wrong. If every conversation starts with what has gone wrong this week, eventually people stop bringing ideas about what might be possible next year.

We then lose much more than capacity. We lose the best thinking of the people inside the system.

Controls can reinforce the problem
#

This is also where my thinking about human judgement, controls and guardrails connects. When something goes wrong, organisations understandably add controls: an additional check, another approval, more evidence, another person who must confirm that a decision is correct. Some of those controls may be essential.

But every control also consumes capacity, adds another potential queue and reinforces attention on the immediate step. If we are not careful, a system designed to prevent yesterday’s mistake gradually removes the organisation’s ability to design tomorrow’s solution.

The question therefore cannot simply be How do we stop this happening again? It also needs to be What would allow this system to operate differently? Those are subtly different questions. One looks primarily backwards. The other preserves the learning from what happened while still allowing us to look forwards.

That is why good guardrails are so powerful. Where rules can be made explicit, automated and embedded into the system, they can provide safety without demanding constant human attention. Human judgement can then be concentrated on the places where judgement genuinely adds value rather than consuming people’s time proving repeatedly that routine decisions remain routine.

We protect our feet without requiring everyone to stare at them.

Sometimes the first intervention is simply visibility
#

Going back to the security-related change example, I am not convinced the first answer is a major technology transformation. It may simply be seeing the work differently.

What kinds of work are actually entering the system? How large are they? How many dependencies do they carry? Where is work waiting rather than being worked on? Which queues are growing? How old is the work in each queue? Which supposedly standard requests generate most of the exceptions?

Once those distinctions become visible, the conversation changes. Instead of asking how to process an enormous homogeneous backlog faster, we can start identifying different flows. Perhaps some requests can be standardised. Perhaps others can be automated. Perhaps high-complexity work needs a completely different route. Perhaps one approval is the real constraint. Perhaps we discover that the team itself is not the bottleneck at all.

Visibility gives us distance from the individual ticket, and distance gives us the opportunity to think.

Look up
#

There is a temptation when organisations are under pressure to demand greater focus. Sometimes that is exactly what is needed. But focus can become myopia.

A team can become extraordinarily good at concentrating on the work immediately in front of it while gradually losing sight of why that work exists, how the surrounding system behaves, and where it is ultimately trying to go. That is particularly dangerous because it can look like commitment. Everyone is busy. Everyone is concentrating. Everyone is trying incredibly hard not to make mistakes. Yet the organisation may still be moving slowly, because nobody has enough capacity to look up.

There is value in watching your feet. It stops you tripping over the next obstacle. But at some point you have to raise your eyes.

Because if you spend the entire journey looking at your toes, you may avoid a few stones along the way. You just might not get where you intended to go.
